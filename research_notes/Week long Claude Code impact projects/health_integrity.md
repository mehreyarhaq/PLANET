## Candidate 1 — Retracted papers still cited in Canadian clinical practice guidelines and government health advice

### Takeaway
Guideline-level "contamination" studies are a crowded, well-funded international niche (VITALITY I in The BMJ 2025, VITALITY II protocol 2026, Besançon–Cabanac–Weber-Boer at Peer Review Congress 2025, Kataoka 2022), but I found **no Canadian-specific, recommendation-level audit** of CTFPHC, CCS, Diabetes Canada, Ontario Health/CCO, Health Canada/PHAC, CMAJ guidelines or Choosing Wisely Canada; the data (Crossref/Retraction Watch CSV, OpenAlex, open-access CMAJ guideline PDFs) is free and the loop is tractable in a week, so this is an **open niche within a crowded field**, with the caveat that the headline depends on finding at least one recommendation that genuinely rests on retracted evidence.

### Cited Findings

**(1) Has it been done?**
- Besançon, Cabanac and Weber-Boer (Peer Review Congress 2025, poster, Chicago, 4 Sept 2025) analysed clinical guidelines published 1991–2025 and found 398 unique guideline–retracted-publication pairs cited after the retraction date; only 2 retracted references were cited critically and none acknowledged the retracted status — [Peer Review Congress abstract](https://peerreviewcongress.org/abstract/retracted-publications-referenced-in-clinical-guidelines/). The "~20,000 guidelines" figure in the brief did not appear in the snippet I could see; treat it as unverified.
- Cabanac and Weber-Boer separately tracked post-retraction citations across biomedical and clinical sciences using a Dimensions snapshot from June 2025 (citing publications rising from 674 in 2005 to 13,927) — [Peer Review Congress abstract](https://peerreviewcongress.org/abstract/postretraction-references-in-biomedical-and-clinical-sciences/).
- Florez et al. (Peer Review Congress 2025) examined retracted systematic reviews and retracted guidelines themselves, finding retracted CPGs continue to be cited after retraction, mainly in background sections — [Peer Review Congress abstract](https://peerreviewcongress.org/abstract/retraction-of-systematic-reviews-and-clinical-practice-guidelines/).
- Kataoka et al. 2022 (J Clin Epidemiol, doi 10.1016/j.jclinepi.2022.06.015; medRxiv preprint): only 5% of SRs (6/130) and 11% of CPGs (2/18) that cited later-retracted RCTs corrected themselves — [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.30.22270124). A related Japanese analysis found 127 reviews and guidelines citing already-retracted trials without caution, none corrected over two years — [Gavi/The Conversation](https://www.gavi.org/vaccineswork/retracted-papers-are-used-clinical-guidelines-how-worried-should-we-be).
- VITALITY Study I (Xu, Furuya-Kanamori, Glasziou et al., The BMJ 2025): using Retraction Watch data to 5 Nov 2024, 1,330 retracted RCTs, 847 SRs, 3,902 replicable meta-analyses; removing retracted trials reversed conclusions in ~4%, changed effect direction in 8.4%, changed significance in 16.0%; 218 meta-analyses changed, affecting 68 SRs and **157 guideline documents** — [BMJ VOR via UEA eprints](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/99702/1/bmj-2024-082068.pdf); news summary [AusDoc](https://www.ausdoc.com.au/news/157-clinical-practice-guidelines-contaminated-by-retracted-studies-say-researchers/). A BMJ rapid response argues counting affected guidelines is insufficient and clinical importance of outcomes should be weighted — [BMJ rapid response](https://www-bmj-com.bibliotheek.ehb.be/content/389/bmj-2024-082068/rr-0).
- VITALITY Study II protocol (BMJ Open 2026, e115303) aims to test how problematic evidence weakens the credibility of CPG *recommendations*, in three work packages — [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/16/3/e115303). A 2026 medRxiv secondary analysis of the VITALITY database found 426 retracted trials led to contamination, 251 events after retraction vs 855 before — [medRxiv 2026](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.02.20.26346631v2). A White Rose (Leeds) protocol plans a cohort of guidelines contaminated by retracted trials/meta-analyses; nothing in the snippet says it covers Canada — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/242186/).
- Canada-specific: my searches ("retracted studies cited Canadian clinical practice guidelines", "Canadian medical guideline cites retracted study CBC Toronto Star") returned no Canadian audit. The only Canadian retraction-meets-guidance story found was the Toronto Star's coverage of Canadian Family Physician and Canadian Pharmacists Journal retracting Motherisk/Gideon Koren columns about a Toronto newborn's death that had prompted agencies worldwide to caution against codeine for nursing mothers — [Toronto Star PDF via Hypertension Canada](https://hypertension.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/We-did-the-right-thing_-Medical-journals-are-retracting-columns-on-a-Toronto-newborns-death-after-reviews-conclude-the-findings-were-unreliable-_-The-Star.pdf). That is a precedent for local-press interest, not a prior audit.
- Institutional responses exist elsewhere: Cochrane now adds a warning to reviews citing retracted papers and asks authors to revise; ICMJE recommends editors check for post-retraction citations — [The Publication Plan](https://thepublicationplan.com/2023/01/12/retracted-papers-are-we-doing-enough-to-put-discredited-research-to-rest/). Prescrire (2025) notes guideline developers "do not always run checks for retracted articles" — [Prescrire](https://english.prescrire.org/EN/81/168/69497/0/NewsDetails.aspx?page=5).

**(2) Data access**
- The Retraction Watch database is distributed by Crossref as a CSV in a GitLab repo (gitlab.com/crossref/retraction-watch-data), updated once per working day; clone and `git pull` — [Crossref documentation](https://crossref.org/documentation/retrieve-metadata/retraction-watch/); [Crossref blog](https://www.crossref.org/blog/retraction-watch-retractions-now-in-the-crossref-api/). Crossref says its metadata is free to reuse without a licence and asks that published work using the RW data cite the source; I could not find a named licence (CC0/CC BY) for the CSV — [Crossref Labs page](https://www.crossref.org/labs/retraction-watch/); README column descriptions mentioned at [Crossref community](https://community.crossref.org/t/ticket-of-the-month-december-2024-retraction-watch-tips/12991). Crossref notes that retraction flags in Crossmark metadata are *not* from Retraction Watch — same source.
- OpenAlex `is_retracted` is a boolean that, between 22 Dec 2023 and 19 Mar 2024, wrongly included corrections and expressions of concern; treat it as a broad flag and confirm against Crossref/RW — [Hauschke & Nazarovets 2025](https://elibrary.kubg.edu.ua/id/eprint/51506/).
- Guideline reference lists: no source found that provides Canadian guideline references as structured data; the loop must parse PDFs/HTML (CMAJ guidelines and CTFPHC are open access on cmaj.ca; others vary). This is an inference, not a cited finding.

**(3) Impact pathway / verifier**
- Verifiers would be the guideline body itself, CMAJ editors, or the retracted paper's journal; VITALITY's own numbers (157 guideline documents) show the press framing "retracted studies contaminate N guidelines" gets picked up — [AusDoc](https://www.ausdoc.com.au/news/157-clinical-practice-guidelines-contaminated-by-retracted-studies-say-researchers/).
- Evidence that guideline bodies rarely respond on their own: 11% CPG correction rate — [medRxiv/Kataoka](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.30.22270124).

**(4) Legal / ethical**
- PubPeer commenters have been sued for defamation (Sarkar v. Does; a Michigan court ordered partial release of identifying information) — [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/litigation/sarkar-v-does/). In Sept 2025 Taylor & Francis threatened legal action against the anonymous "ScienceGuardians" group over "highly defamatory" claims about sleuths; Elisabeth Bik notes sleuths use pseudonyms out of fear of being sued — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/09/26/taylor-francis-threatens-legal-action-against-anonymous-groups-highly-defamatory-claims/).
- Ontario has an anti-SLAPP provision (s.137.1 Courts of Justice Act); the Court of Appeal in Gill v. Maciver, 2024 ONCA 126 dismissed a vaccine-sceptic doctor's suit against 23 doctors/journalists as an attempt to limit debate on a matter of public interest — [Law Times](https://www.lawtimesnews.com/practice-areas/litigation/covid-vaccine-skeptic-doctor-loses-anti-slapp-case-at-court-of-appeal/384083); an Ontario Superior Court (25 Nov 2025) dismissed a $2M defamation claim by a clinical-stage pharma company as an "archetypical SLAPP" and awarded the defendant $25,000 — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/canada/libel-defamation/1750648/online-criticism-and-defamation-an-ontario-anti-slapp-decision).

### Inferences
- **Week plan.** Day 1: clone RW CSV; build DOI/PMID/title index of all retractions (plus EoCs, kept separately). Day 1–2: enumerate the Canadian corpus — every CMAJ-published guideline and CTFPHC recommendation statement (open access), CCS/Diabetes Canada/Ontario Health–CCO/Choosing Wisely Canada/Health Canada–PHAC guidance documents — and extract reference lists (PDF text → DOI/title fuzzy match via Crossref/OpenAlex). Day 2–3: match references against retractions; record retraction date vs guideline date (post-retraction citation is the stronger finding). Day 3–5: for each hit, have the agent locate *which recommendation* cites it, whether it is the sole supporting evidence, and whether the recommendation is current; re-run any cited meta-analysis with the trial removed where data is tabulated (VITALITY's method). Day 5–7: falsification pass (confirm retraction status on the journal site, check for guideline updates, check whether the guideline cites a later-retracted SR rather than the trial), then write-up with per-recommendation evidence tables.
- **Realism.** VITALITY found 157 guideline documents affected globally; with Canada's share of guidelines, a handful of hits is plausible, and the "post-retraction citation, no acknowledgement" pattern (Besançon: 0/398 acknowledged) suggests any Canadian hits will be unflagged. The risky part is the recommendation-level dependency: most hits will be background citations, so the headline may degrade to "N Canadian guidelines cite retracted papers" unless at least one load-bearing case is found.
- **Likely headline.** "Canadian clinical guidelines still cite retracted studies — and nobody flagged it" (CMAJ news / Retraction Watch); a load-bearing case would reach CBC/Toronto Star given the Motherisk precedent.
- **Legal posture.** Naming a guideline body and a retracted paper is low risk (retraction is a public fact); avoid characterising the *authors* of retracted papers beyond the published retraction notice; route findings to the guideline body and CMAJ before publishing; Ontario anti-SLAPP law helps but does not stop a demand letter.
- **Saturation rating: open niche inside a crowded field.** Crowded globally (VITALITY I/II, Cabanac group, Kataoka, Leeds protocol); Canadian recommendation-level audit **not found** — this rests on absence of search evidence across four queries.

### Gaps
- Could not read the Besançon abstract in full (sample size, whether Canadian guidelines are in its corpus, and whether per-guideline data is released); if their dataset is public the Canadian subset could be extracted in a day.
- Licence terms of the RW CSV (named licence) not found; check the GitLab README/LICENSE.
- No source found on whether Canadian guideline bodies (CTFPHC, CCS, Diabetes Canada) have any retraction-checking policy.

## Candidate 2 — Unreported clinical trials by Canadian sponsors/institutions (TrialsTracker-style league table)

### Takeaway
The Canadian aggregate problem is well documented (FACETS 2023: 32% of 6,720 Canada-site trials had neither registry results nor a publication; only 3% of Canada-only trials met all three criteria) and has had CBC/CTV/CMAJ coverage, but **no current institution-level Canadian league table** exists (the only one is a 2016 CBC/TrialsTracker story and a 2019 McGill editorial with figures like U of T 64% unreported); CIHR publishes only anonymous aggregate compliance. Data is free (ClinicalTrials.gov API v2, no key) and the loop is feasible; the main risks are the "unpublished vs. unfindable" false-positive problem TranspariMED has warned about, and the fact that most Canadian academic trials have no legal duty to post results on ClinicalTrials.gov.

### Cited Findings

**(1) Has it been done?**
- Alayche et al., FACETS 2023: all ClinicalTrials.gov trials conducted in Canada registered by 2009 and completed by 2019 — 6,720 trials; 59% (3,967) prospectively registered; 32% (2,138) had neither results reported nor findings published; among 3,763 Canada-only trials, 3% (123) met all three criteria — [FACETS via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/2a3bcee7287743a1843053326ce14812); [UBC Therapeutics Initiative summary](https://www.ti.ubc.ca/?p=10798); [OHRI newsroom](https://ohri.ca/en/newsroom/canada-isnt-registering-and-reporting-results-all-clinical-trials-heres-how-fix). Press: CTV News "Patients 'betrayed'…" — [CTV](https://ctvnews.ca/health/this-harms-patients-it-wastes-public-money-it-s-clearly-unethical-study-of-how-much-medical-research-actually-meets-the-criteria-1.6655870); CMAJ news "Canadian funders and institutions are lagging on reporting results" — [CMAJ](https://www.cmaj.ca/custom-print/161120).
- Phase IV trials with a Canadian site 2000–2022 (Lexchin co-author, IJHPM 2024): publication 81.8% industry vs 65.8% non-industry — [IJHPM](https://www.ijhpm.com/UfToZ/article_4576.html).
- Institution-level figures exist only in older form: CBC (24 Nov 2016, Darryl Hol) used the Oxford TrialsTracker to report that Canadian universities and research hospitals (UBC, University Health Network named) were among the worst offenders; both institutions said the algorithm misses some results — [CAREB summary of CBC story](https://careb-accer.org/cbc-news-article-re-results-missing-from-clinical-trials-registered-by-canadian-universities-and-research-hospitals/); [UBC News 2016](https://news.ubc.ca/2016/11/25/many-clinical-trial-results-are-never-published/). A 2019 McGill Journal of Medicine editorial cites McMaster 46%, University of Toronto 64%, UBC 74% unreported (journals + registry) and 91% of McGill trials completed before Dec 2017 lacking ClinicalTrials.gov summary results — [MJM](https://mjm.mcgill.ca/article/download/145/38). TrialsTracker methodology paper — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5200941/).
- No TranspariMED ranking of Canadian institutions found (they have US, European, Nordic reports); a TranspariMED post argues CIHR should publicly identify noncompliant sponsors rather than publishing anonymous compliance data — [TranspariMED Canada regulation post](https://www.transparimed.org/single-post/canada-clinical-trials-regulation); [TranspariMED on CIHR](https://www.transparimed.org/single-post/canada-cihr-clinical-trials). TranspariMED also warns that standard methodologies for counting unreported trials may overstate the problem — [TranspariMED "metascience fail"](https://www.transparimed.org/single-post/metascience-fail-four-lessons-from-inaccurate-data-on-missing-clinical-trial-results).
- Policy: CIHR's Clinical Trial Registration and Results Disclosure Policy applies to NPIs funded on/after 1 Jan 2022; CIHR monitors annually, reports aggregate data, and may withhold new funding — [CIHR policy](https://cihr-irsc.gc.ca/e/54747.html). The 2024 monitoring cycle covered 456 trials; 98.9% of NPIs responded; 74.5% had registered; 97.8% had not yet published results — [CIHR 2024 monitoring PDF](https://cihr.ca/e/documents/monitoring_clinical_trials_registration_disclosure_2024-en.pdf). (A 2025 cycle reportedly shows 80.0% registered and 94.8% not yet published, with enforcement planned after full analysis; the snippet did not give a distinct URL — see Gaps.)
- Regulation: Vanessa's Law s.21.71 gave only a power to make regulations on trial registration/results; a 2024 CMAJ analysis says those regulations have never been published; Health Canada issued draft guidance in Feb 2023 that *encourages* registration and results publication — [CMAJ 2024](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/196/18/E628); [PMC version](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11090641); Health Canada transparency page says remaining authorities come into force only when supporting regulations are published — [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/legislation-guidelines/transparency-measures-protecting-canadians-unsafe-drugs-act-vanessa-law-amendments-food-drugs-act.html).

**(2) Data access**
- ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 at https://clinicaltrials.gov/api/v2 (OpenAPI 3.0; JSON/CSV; `/studies` with `query.*`/`filter.*` params, `pageToken` pagination; `/studies/{nctId}`; no API key); a third-party doc cites ~50 requests/min/IP but no official rate limit was found; official docs at clinicaltrials.gov/data-api/about-api (not opened) — [DEV Community](https://dev.to/ronin13/clinical-trials-data-has-a-free-json-api-every-study-on-clinicaltrialsgov-no-key-required-1pek); [IntuitionLabs pipeline guide](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/clinicaltrials-gov-api-v2-pipeline); [BioMCP reference](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@genomoncology/biomcp/blob/c7db7e1ea47c1df8f8f9bfc98a5bf2da5c962e3a/docs/backend-services-reference/04-clinicaltrials-gov.md).
- Health Canada's Clinical Trials Database is "not a registry" and not comprehensive; it has JSON/XML API endpoints by sponsor, medical condition and drug product via Open Canada, and a web search page; no whole-database download found — [Open Canada dataset](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/d6fe4b32-2eaf-4ac0-9e35-b3841f25e3a7); [Health Canada CTD page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/health-canada-clinical-trials-database.html); [search tool](https://health-products.canada.ca/ctdb-bdec/).
- Legal scope caveat: FDAAA 801 results-posting applies to "applicable clinical trials" with a US site or conducted under an IND/IDE; trials with no US site and no IND are *not* legally required to post results on ClinicalTrials.gov — [AGG summary](https://www.agg.com/news-insights/publications/lets-get-clinical-clinicaltrialsgov-reporting-requirements-now-in-effect-05-18-2017/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2821287). A 2026 blog says FDA sent compliance letters to >2,200 sponsors (unverified, secondary) — [journalmetrics](https://www.journalmetrics.org/blog/clinicaltrials-gov-results-reporting-requirements-2026).

**(3) Impact pathway / verifier**
- Precedent coverage: CBC 2016 (TrialsTracker), CTV 2023, CMAJ news; TranspariMED actively campaigns on Canada — sources above. CIHR's own aggregate monitoring is the natural institutional verifier; TranspariMED's critique is that it is anonymous — [TranspariMED on CIHR](https://www.transparimed.org/single-post/canada-cihr-clinical-trials).

**(4) Legal / ethical**
- Naming institutions for unposted results is standard practice (TrialsTracker, TranspariMED, CBC); the known ethical pitfall is false positives — UBC and UHN both told CBC the algorithm misses results — [CAREB/CBC](https://careb-accer.org/cbc-news-article-re-results-missing-from-clinical-trials-registered-by-canadian-universities-and-research-hospitals/); TranspariMED's "metascience fail" post documents overstated counts — [TranspariMED](https://www.transparimed.org/single-post/metascience-fail-four-lessons-from-inaccurate-data-on-missing-clinical-trial-results).

### Inferences
- **Week plan.** Day 1: pull every ClinicalTrials.gov interventional study with `locationCountry = Canada` (or lead sponsor in Canada), status Completed/Terminated, primary completion ≥2 years ago (~thousands); store JSON. Day 2: classify sponsor class and map sites to Toronto institutions (UHN, SickKids, Sunnybrook, Unity Health, Mount Sinai, CAMH, U of T) via affiliation strings. Day 2–4: for each trial without posted results, search PubMed (NCT ID in secondary source ID; title/PI/condition), Europe PMC, medRxiv, Google Scholar; record best-match publication and confidence. Day 4–5: build per-institution table of (registered, results posted, published, neither); compute the CIHR-funded subset using CIHR's funding database. Day 5–6: falsification — manual spot-check a random 50 "unreported" trials, estimate the false-negative rate, and report the table with that error band (this answers the TranspariMED critique). Day 7: write-up and send to the institutions for comment before publication.
- **Realism.** High: all steps are API/search operations; the per-trial literature search is the bottleneck but an agent can run ~thousands over a week. The product is a *current* Toronto/Canada league table with a measured error rate, which does not exist.
- **Likely headline.** "X% of trials run at Toronto's hospitals never reported results — patients enrolled, answers never shared" (Toronto Star/CBC Toronto); CMAJ news; TranspariMED would amplify.
- **Legal posture.** Low defamation risk if the method is transparent and institutions are given a right of reply; do not call unreported trials "hidden" or imply intent; distinguish "no legal duty" (non-FDAAA trials) from "ethical duty" (CIHR policy, WHO joint statement).
- **Saturation rating: some competition.** Aggregate Canadian numbers are published (FACETS 2023, Phase IV 2024) and CIHR monitors its own trials; an institution-level, current table was **not found** (rests on absence of evidence in three queries; the only institution-level data is 2016–2019).

### Gaps
- Official ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 rate limits and bulk-snapshot location not confirmed from clinicaltrials.gov itself.
- The CIHR 2025 monitoring figures (80.0%/94.8%) were surfaced in a snippet without a distinct URL; confirm on cihr-irsc.gc.ca.
- Whether any Canadian group (e.g., the Ottawa/OHRI FACETS team) is currently building an institution-level tracker is unknown.

## Candidate 3 — Pharmacovigilance signal mining in Canada Vigilance (disproportionality vs Canadian product monographs and FAERS)

### Takeaway
Canada Vigilance bulk extracts are free, monthly, and under-used (I found descriptive studies and a few small PRR/ROR/BCPNN studies, no comprehensive all-pairs scan cross-checked against Canadian monographs), so the niche is **open (unverified)** — but the 2026 Drug Safety review and the READUS-PV perspective make clear that disproportionality findings from an individual carry little weight unless cross-validated (FAERS, label text, biological plausibility) and framed as hypotheses; Health Canada's Summary Safety Review → monograph-update pathway is the realistic verifier.

### Cited Findings

**(1) Has it been done?**
- Descriptive/academic uses of Canada Vigilance: Maity & Longo 2020 (ADRs 2009–2018, descriptive) — [Healthcare Quarterly](https://www.longwoods.com/content/26140); Al Dweik et al. 2020 (198,781 reports 2000–2014, consumer vs physician) — [Drugs & Therapy Perspectives PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40267-020-00762-6.pdf); a McMaster dissertation using Canada Vigilance for Remicade/Humira serious outcomes — [MacSphere](https://macsphere.mcmaster.ca/handle/11375/31497). Small disproportionality studies on Canadian data: platinum compounds (PRR, ROR, chi-square, BCPNN; thresholds chi-square ≥4, others ≥3) — [IJPPS](https://journals.innovareacademics.in/index.php/ijpps/article/view/5025); propranolol bradycardia class-specific detection — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2875734/). My queries for "Canada Vigilance disproportionality/reporting odds ratio 2025" found no comprehensive all-drug–all-event scan.
- Health Canada's process: Canada Vigilance collects voluntary HCP/consumer reports and mandatory manufacturer reports and analyses them for signals — [Health Canada program page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/reports-publications/medeffect-canada/canada-vigilance-program-collecting-assessing-adverse-reaction-reports-health-canada-2011.html). A 2018 internal-audit response says Health Canada was revising processes, building IT for data-mining signal detection, and planned to procure a data-mining tool in Jan 2019, combining domestic reports with international data — [Health Canada management response](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/corporate/transparency/corporate-management-reporting/internal-audits/biologics-pharmaceutical-drugs-program/management-response-action-plan.html). Current algorithms/thresholds were not found.
- Verifier pathway in practice: Health Product InfoWatch issues show safety reviews concluding "possible link" followed by monograph updates — e.g., GLP-1 RA class monograph update (April 2026), Givlaari acute pancreatitis (June 2026), Stivarga label change reflecting a Summary Safety Review (March 2026) — [InfoWatch March 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/health-product-infowatch/march-2026.html); [April 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/health-product-infowatch/april-2026.html); [June 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/health-product-infowatch/june-2026.html).

**(2) Data access**
- Canada Vigilance data extract: monthly; coverage listed as 1965 to 2025-11-30 (snippet; may be cached — see Gaps); 11 `$`-delimited quoted flat files; "intended for users familiar with database structures"; vaccine reports only since 1 Jan 2011 — [Data extract page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/adverse-reaction-database/canada-vigilance-online-database-data-extract.html); [Data structure page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/adverse-reaction-database/canada-vigilance-adverse-reaction-online-database-data-structures.html); [Open Canada listing](https://open.canada.ca/data/en/dataset/9cbaef00-b52c-4a70-9fed-d9aa8263ab74). Health Canada's caveat: report counts must not be used to estimate incidence or risk, and the database "cannot be used on its own to evaluate a health product's safety profile" — [Caveat/privacy page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/adverse-reaction-database/medeffect-canada-caveat-privacy-statement-interpretation-data-extract-vigilance-adverse-reaction-online-database.html). Licence not named in snippets (expected Open Government Licence – Canada; unverified). No GitHub/PyPI loader for the extract was found.
- Canadian product monographs: Drug Product Database extract (compressed UTF-8 CSV-style files; approved/marketed/cancelled/dormant; updated regularly, last listed 2026-03-02) — [DPD extracts](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/drug-product-database/extracts.html); [Read Me](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/drug-product-database/read-file-drug-product-database-data-extract.html). The extract does not obviously include monograph text; monographs are PDFs per product, and Health Canada keeps a "Product monograph brand safety updates" list — [Label safety assessment update page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/drug-product-database/label-safety-assessment-update.html).
- FAERS: quarterly ASCII/SGML files (now under the "AEMS" banner), non-cumulative, requiring relational-DB skills; NBER mirrors in CSV/Stata — [FDA FAERS quarterly files](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/fda-adverse-event-reporting-system-faers/fda-adverse-event-reporting-system-faers-latest-quarterly-data-files); [NBER](https://www.nber.org/research/data/fda-adverse-event-reporting-system).

**(3) Statistical pitfalls / would an individual be taken seriously?**
- Fusaroli et al. 2026 (Drug Safety): disproportionality analysis is often misapplied; confounding, notoriety bias, masking, misclassification, violated independence; sophisticated methods add collider bias and masking amplification; DA should be supportive, not decisive — [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:da02c52b-1278-4f73-b25e-6eaa71bc1aa4/files/rzs25xb90p). Hammad et al. 2025 (Frontiers): many DA publications make causal statements not supported by data; premature publication of spontaneous-report signals adds overinterpretation risk — [Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2025.1573353/full). FDA: spurious alerts from concomitant exposure, confounding by indication, stimulated reporting; absence of disproportionality does not rule out a signal — [FDA data mining](https://www.fda.gov/science-research/data-mining/data-mining-center-biologics-evaluation-and-research). Litigation-associated reports: ~4% of FAERS signals appeared only when LARs were excluded — [RAPS](https://www.raps.org/resource/fda-officials-explain-when-litigation-can-impact-d.html). Solicited reports can mask true signals and create false positives — [Jokinen 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6739274). 2026 methods review (incl. empirical Bayes) — [arXiv 2604.18898](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18898).

**(4) Legal / ethical**
- Canada Vigilance extracts are de-identified; the caveat page governs interpretation (no incidence claims) — [Caveat page](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/adverse-reaction-database/medeffect-canada-caveat-privacy-statement-interpretation-data-extract-vigilance-adverse-reaction-online-database.html). Naming a drug–event "signal" publicly can affect patients and manufacturers; the READUS-PV perspective's warning about causal language applies — [Frontiers](https://frontiersin.org/journals/pharmacology/articles/10.3389/fphar.2025.1573353/full).

### Inferences
- **Week plan.** Day 1: download and load the 11 files into SQLite/DuckDB; normalise drug names to active ingredients (DPD extract gives DIN→ingredient). Day 2: compute PRR/ROR/IC (BCPNN) for all ingredient × MedDRA PT pairs with standard thresholds, stratified by year and reporter type; keep only pairs with ≥5 reports and ≥2 years of data. Day 3: for the top few hundred, scrape the current Canadian monograph PDF and test whether the PT (or a synonym) appears in Adverse Reactions/Warnings; also check the FDA label (DailyMed) and FAERS disproportionality for the same pair. Day 4–5: falsification — remove litigation/stimulated-reporting years, check indication confounding (is the event the disease?), check class effects, check whether a Summary Safety Review already exists. Day 6: rank the residual "unlabelled in Canada, labelled in the US / strong in both databases" pairs. Day 7: write a hypothesis-framed note and send to Health Canada's Marketed Health Products Directorate and the manufacturer.
- **Realism.** Computing the full table is a day of laptop work; the monograph cross-check is the novel part and the slow part (PDF text, synonyms). A "labelled in the US but not in Canada, and disproportionate in Canada Vigilance" list is a realistic, defensible week-one output; a brand-new causal signal is not.
- **Likely headline.** "Health Canada's own adverse-event data flags side effects missing from Canadian drug labels" — plausible for CBC/Toronto Star only if Health Canada or an independent pharmacologist confirms; otherwise this reads as an undergraduate's hypothesis list. Retraction Watch is not the venue; a pharmacovigilance journal letter or Health Canada's response is.
- **Saturation rating: open (unverified).** No comprehensive Canadian all-pairs scan or Canada-vs-label audit found in four queries; but the field's methodological scepticism (Fusaroli 2026) and Health Canada's in-house data mining mean the *impact* bar is high. This rating rests on absence of search evidence.

## Candidate 4 — Agent-driven reproducibility audit of a defined corpus with data and code

### Takeaway
2025–2026 saw an explosion of agent-reproduction benchmarks and large audits (PaperBench, CORE-Bench "solved" by Claude 4.5 Opus, REPRO-Bench, SocSci-Repro-Bench with Claude Code beating Codex, AuditOwl's audit of 100 NeurIPS 2025 papers, Xu & Yang's 384-study political-science replication, ReproRepo's 1,149 papers, Brodeur et al.'s 110-paper Nature 2026 study), so the generic "AI agents tried to reproduce N papers" story is **crowded**; the tractable, unclaimed corpus for a Canadian angle is the Canadian Journal of Economics (mandatory DCAS-compliant packages on Borealis Dataverse), whereas CMAJ Open closed in Jan 2024 and medical DAS "available on request" statements are 93% unfulfilled, making a medical corpus infeasible in a week.

### Cited Findings

**(1) State of the art 2025–2026**
- PaperBench: from-scratch replication of ML papers — [arXiv 2504.01848](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.01848). CORE-Bench: run existing CodeOcean capsules, selection-biased toward reproducible projects — [arXiv 2409.11363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.11363); a blog reports Claude 4.5 Opus "solves" CORE-Bench but not REPRO-Bench — [ddkang substack](https://ddkang.substack.com/p/claude-45-opus-solves-core-bench). AEA Data Editor Vilhuber's team has checked >2,400 replication packages; no evidence found of AEA using LLMs — [Cowles/Yale](https://cowles.yale.edu/events/2025/best-practices-reproducible-science-lessons-2400-replication-packages-prof-lars-vilhuber).

## Adjacent idea B — statcheck-style statistical-consistency audit of Canadian medical journals (CMAJ, CJC, CJPH, CJS, CJA) 2024–2026

### Takeaway
statcheck has shown half of psychology papers contain a p-value inconsistent with its test statistic and 1 in 8 a gross one; psychiatry journals show 14.3% inconsistent results; I found **no statcheck-style audit of medical journals in Canada or of CMAJ**, and open-access CMAJ full text makes this a one-week, low-legal-risk, PubPeer-ready loop — **open (unverified)**.

### Cited Findings
- Nuijten et al. 2016: >250,000 p-values from eight psychology journals 1985–2013; half of NHST papers had ≥1 inconsistent p-value; 1 in 8 had a gross inconsistency; gross inconsistencies more common for results reported as significant — [PMC5101263](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5101263). Psychiatry replication: 78/546 (14.3%) inconsistent; refers to a BMJ/Nature survey finding inconsistent statistics common — [ANU portal](https://researchportalplus.anu.edu.au/en/publications/inconsistencies-between-reported-test-statistics-and-p-values-in-/). statcheck extended for meta-analysis tests — [PMC7540394](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7540394/).
- Precedent for automated screening feeding PubPeer/Retraction Watch: the Problematic Paper Screener (Cabanac, Labbé, Magazinov) flags papers via Dimensions queries and human assessment; ~19,000 tortured-phrase papers — [arXiv 2210.04895](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.04895); [HAL](https://hal-univ-tlse3.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03603538).

### Inferences
- **Week plan.** Day 1: harvest 2024–2026 open-access full text (CMAJ via PMC; others via Europe PMC where OA). Day 2–4: extract test statistics, df, p-values, CIs, and recompute (statcheck for APA-style; LLM-assisted extraction for medical-style reporting, which statcheck's regex misses); also GRIM/SPRITE checks on reported means with integer data. Day 5: falsify (one-sided tests, rounding, Bonferroni, exact vs asymptotic). Day 6: classify gross inconsistencies that flip significance; Day 7: post neutral PubPeer notes and send to editors.
- **Impact.** Retraction Watch/CMAJ editorial interest is plausible if the gross-inconsistency rate is high; local press less so. Legal risk is low if comments are factual and non-accusatory (PubPeer norms), but authors are named by implication, so phrasing matters.
- **Saturation: open (unverified)** — absence of search evidence for a medical/Canadian statcheck audit (one query).

### Gaps
- Medical-journal statcheck rates (BMJ/Nature survey) not retrieved; statcheck's recall on non-APA medical reporting unknown.
