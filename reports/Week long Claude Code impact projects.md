# Start with Ontario's lost gas wells

The best week-long, non-astronomy project for a Toronto mathematics undergraduate with a laptop and a continuously running Claude Code loop is a scan of century-old topographic maps, 1954 air photos and Ontario's open LiDAR terrain model for the unrecorded oil and gas wells the province admits it cannot find: every input is open or free to a U of T student, the US Department of Energy has already proven the map-reading method (**1,301 candidate wells across four counties**), Ontario is spending **$23.6 million** on legacy wells after the 2021 Wheatley explosion, and no Ontario attempt surfaced in any search. Seven more loops clear the same bar of novelty, week-feasibility, a named verifier and public impact: an accessibility league table of every AODA-covered Ontario public-sector website; a current unreported-trials table for Toronto's hospitals with a measured error rate; a diff of every Canadian product monograph against its FDA label for missing boxed warnings; a reconciliation of roughly 650 municipal drinking-water reports against the ministry's own incident extract; an overlay of Toronto's georeferenced Goad fire-insurance plans on today's schools and daycares; a recommendation-level audit of Canadian clinical guidelines for retracted evidence; and a dead-cross-reference sweep of Ontario's and Canada's consolidated statutes. The ideas to drop are the ones whose data is contractually closed (marketplace scraping for recalled products), physically too coarse (TROPOMI methane attribution), already run weekly by someone else (Courtready's hallucinated-citation database), or mined to exhaustion (federal procurement, political donations). Every "open niche" rating here rests on search snippets alone, because no page could be fetched during this round, so the first day of the week belongs to a re-check and an email to the named verifier.

## Eight loops ranked: the wells first, the statute sweep last

The ranking weighs novelty (is there evidence nobody has done this), week-feasibility (does a continuous agent loop on a laptop with free data produce a finding in seven days), verifiability (is there a named official who will check and act) and impact (public safety, health, environment, public money, rule of law or access), in that order; a Toronto or Ontario angle breaks ties. Saturation uses four grades: **crowded** (funded groups or a published sweep already occupy the space), **some competition** (a small circle does this; room for a careful newcomer), **open niche** (the method is proven elsewhere and no attempt was found for this target), and **open (unverified)** (nobody was found but the specific confirmatory search could not be run). Each day-by-day plan is the researchers' inference, not a sourced schedule.

### 1. Unrecorded oil and gas wells in southwestern Ontario

**Finding.** A ranked list of candidate wells in Lambton, Chatham-Kent, Essex, Norfolk, Brant and Haldimand that appear as derrick symbols on 1906–1930s topographic sheets, as pads, berms or access tracks in the LiDAR terrain model, or as derricks and tank batteries in 1954 air photos, yet sit more than 100 m from any well in the provincial database, plus clusters of water-well records that mention gas with no recorded petroleum well nearby. **Already done?** No. Ontario has about **27,000 recorded wells against at least 50,000 drilled**, and "spotty record keeping means there could be tens of thousands of unrecorded and lost wells" ([The Narwhal](https://thenarwhal.ca/ontario-old-oil-gas-wells-explainer/)); Brant County alone has 269 records, about 100 with missing information ([CTV Kitchener](https://www.ctvnews.ca/kitchener/article/thousands-of-abandoned-oil-wells-could-pose-danger-to-ontario-landowners-including-brant-county/)). The method is proven: Lawrence Berkeley Lab trained a U-Net on well symbols in old USGS maps, flagged symbols more than 100 m from documented wells, produced **1,301 potential undocumented wells across four counties**, and verified 29 by satellite and 15 by field magnetometry ([Berkeley Lab](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2024/12/04/ai-helps-researchers-dig-through-old-maps-to-find-lost-oil-and-gas-wells/)). CBC Windsor's interactive map covers only wells already in the Ontario Petroleum Data System ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/1.6160949)), and searches for Ontario undocumented-well LiDAR or machine-learning work returned nothing Ontario-specific. **Data and access.** Petroleum Wells under the Open Government Licence – Ontario as FGDB, shapefile and REST service ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/en/dataset/petroleum-wells)); the lidar-derived Ontario DTM in 1 km tiles under the same licence ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/ontario-digital-terrain-model-lidar-derived)); more than 1,000 OCUL historical topographic sheets at 600 ppi that "can be downloaded for any purpose" ([OCUL](https://ocul.on.ca/topomaps/about-project)); U of T's georeferenced 1954 Hunting Survey air photos, free to the U of T community ([U of T MDL](https://mdl.library.utoronto.ca/node/40973)); and the quarterly open Water Well Information System ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/well-records)). **The week.** Day 1: merge the provincial well table with the OGSR Library's free CSV and define the area of interest. Day 2: pull the OCUL sheets, run a symbol detector (template matching or a small U-Net on the Berkeley pattern), georeference every hit. Day 3: download DTM tiles over the hits and over known well clusters, compute hillshade and local relief, train a pad classifier with known wells as positives. Day 4: overlay the 1954 photos and cross-check Brant's records. Day 5: score candidates by concordance across layers and distance from recorded wells. Day 6: falsification, removing water wells, quarries, silos and drainage features, re-running with perturbed thresholds, and mining the WWIS free text for "gas", "H2S" and "odour" as an independent layer. Day 7: per-candidate evidence cards to the ministry before anything is published. **Verifier and pathway.** The MNR Petroleum Operations Section (posrecords@ontario.ca) and the Abandoned Works Program intake (gasandoilwells@ontario.ca), which visits sites, ranks them by risk and pays contractors ([ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/abandoned-works-program)); the program has spent **$29.5 million capping 415 wells at an average of $71,084 each** ([CTV Windsor](https://www.ctvnews.ca/windsor/article/ontario-government-increasing-funding-to-locate-and-plug-old-and-inactive-oil-and-gas-wells/)), the province added $23.6 million under a 2023 action plan ([CTV Windsor](https://windsor.ctvnews.ca/ontario-invests-23-6m-into-province-wide-strategy-addressing-abandoned-oil-and-gas-wells-1.6424966)), and the fire chiefs have pressed the province for exactly this kind of help ([Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/canada/article-ontario-fire-chiefs-press-province-action-wheatley-gas-explosion)). **Headline.** "U of T student's AI scan of century-old maps flags hundreds of possible unrecorded gas wells in southwestern Ontario; ministry says it will check" (CBC Windsor, Toronto Star, The Narwhal). **Main risk.** Landowners become responsible for plugging where no operator exists ([ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/abandoned-works-program)), so a parcel-level public list imposes cost and stigma, and officials warn against anyone investigating a suspected well themselves ([Brant County](https://www.brant.ca/emergency-services-and-health-care/oil-and-gas-wells/)). Berkeley's ratio of 1,301 candidates to 44 verified means most leads will fail, so the deliverable is leads for the ministry, never "wells found". **Saturation.** Open niche for Ontario, absence-based; crowded in the United States and Alberta ([Climate Change AI](https://www.climatechange.ai/papers/neurips2021/64)), which raises credibility rather than lowering novelty.

### 2. An accessibility league table of every AODA-covered Ontario public-sector website

**Finding.** Which of Ontario's 444 municipalities, roughly 140 hospital corporations, 72 school boards, colleges, universities and provincial agencies still fail the WCAG 2.0 Level AA standard their public websites were legally required to meet by 1 January 2021, ranked, with reproducible evidence. **Already done?** Not for this population. O. Reg. 191/11 s. 14 required designated public-sector organizations to make all websites and web content conform to WCAG 2.0 AA by 1 January 2021 ([ontario.ca](https://ontario.ca/page/how-make-websites-accessible)); the 2023 Donovan review called the province's accessibility performance a crisis and no specific reforms followed ([ontario.ca](https://ontario.ca/page/2023-legislative-review-accessibility-ontarians-disabilities-act-2005); [AODA Alliance](https://www.aodaalliance.org/?p=4869)). The only prior scans are a 2022 thesis in which **all 21 Ontario university accessibility-office homepages failed WCAG 2.1** ([MSVU](https://ec.msvu.ca/items/358b7337-a21b-4286-a3d2-90dfd59ed0d8)) and Silktide's automated index, which already ranks Canadian universities (Wilfrid Laurier scored 43 in May 2025) ([Silktide](https://index.silktide.com/website/wilfrid-laurier-university)); nothing covering municipalities, hospitals or school boards surfaced. **Data and access.** Public websites crawled at modest rates with axe-core and Playwright, from official target lists. Automated tools detect only a subset of WCAG failures ([W3C WAI](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/tools/list/)), so the table is a lower bound and must use WCAG 2.0 AA rules only, to match the legal standard. **The week.** Day 1: assemble the target list and resolve homepages. Days 2–3: crawl 20–50 pages per site (headless Chromium handles thousands of pages a day on a laptop). Day 4: agent triage, deduplicating template-level errors, classifying by user impact (missing form labels, keyboard traps, contrast), capturing evidence. Day 5: falsification, re-crawling the top and bottom sites for stability, spot-checking ten with a screen reader, excluding third-party embeds. Day 6: build the table and send the worst twenty organizations their findings for comment. Day 7: write-up. **Verifier and pathway.** The Accessibility Directorate of Ontario, whose enforcement runs from notices to Director's Orders to penalties of up to $100,000 per day for corporations ([aoda.ca](https://www.aoda.ca/who-enforces-aoda-accessibility-compliance/)); the AODA Alliance as amplifier; municipal and hospital accessibility advisory committees, which must respond. **Headline.** "X of Ontario's 444 municipal websites fail the accessibility standard they were legally required to meet in 2021" (Toronto Star, CBC Toronto). **Main risk.** Low: organizations, not people, are named and the findings are mechanically reproducible; respect robots.txt and rate-limit hospital sites. **Saturation.** Open niche (unverified) for municipalities, hospitals and school boards; some competition for universities.

### 3. A current unreported-trials table for Toronto's hospitals, with a measured error rate

**Finding.** For UHN, SickKids, Sunnybrook, Unity Health, Mount Sinai, CAMH and U of T, the share of completed clinical trials that posted no results and have no findable publication, with the false-positive rate of that classification measured on a hand-checked sample. **Already done?** The national aggregate is known: of **6,720 trials with a Canadian site, 32% had neither registry results nor a publication**, and only 3% of 3,763 Canada-only trials met all three transparency criteria ([FACETS via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/2a3bcee7287743a1843053326ce14812); [OHRI](https://ohri.ca/en/newsroom/canada-isnt-registering-and-reporting-results-all-clinical-trials-heres-how-fix)), covered as "patients betrayed" ([CTV](https://ctvnews.ca/health/this-harms-patients-it-wastes-public-money-it-s-clearly-unethical-study-of-how-much-medical-research-actually-meets-the-criteria-1.6655870)). Institution-level numbers exist only from 2016, when CBC used TrialsTracker to name UBC and UHN and both said the algorithm misses results ([CAREB](https://careb-accer.org/cbc-news-article-re-results-missing-from-clinical-trials-registered-by-canadian-universities-and-research-hospitals/)), and a 2019 editorial citing **University of Toronto at 64% unreported** ([McGill Journal of Medicine](https://mjm.mcgill.ca/article/download/145/38)). CIHR monitors only its own trials and publishes anonymous aggregates (74.5% of 456 trials registered, 97.8% not yet published in the 2024 cycle) ([CIHR](https://cihr.ca/e/documents/monitoring_clinical_trials_registration_disclosure_2024-en.pdf)), which TranspariMED says should name sponsors ([TranspariMED](https://www.transparimed.org/single-post/canada-cihr-clinical-trials)). **Data and access.** ClinicalTrials.gov API v2 is JSON, paginated and needs no key ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/ronin13/clinical-trials-data-has-a-free-json-api-every-study-on-clinicaltrialsgov-no-key-required-1pek)); the legal results-posting duty applies only to trials with a US site or IND ([AGG](https://www.agg.com/news-insights/publications/lets-get-clinical-clinicaltrialsgov-reporting-requirements-now-in-effect-05-18-2017/)), so the write-up must separate legal from ethical duty. **The week.** Day 1: pull every interventional study with a Canadian location, Completed or Terminated, primary completion at least two years ago. Day 2: classify sponsors and map sites to Toronto institutions. Days 2–4: for each trial without posted results, search PubMed, Europe PMC, medRxiv and Google Scholar by NCT number, title, investigator and condition, recording the best match and a confidence score. Days 4–5: build the per-institution table and the CIHR-funded subset. Days 5–6: falsification, hand-checking a random 50 "unreported" trials to estimate the miss rate and reporting the table with that error band, which answers the 2016 rebuttal and TranspariMED's warning that standard counts overstate the problem ([TranspariMED](https://www.transparimed.org/single-post/metascience-fail-four-lessons-from-inaccurate-data-on-missing-clinical-trial-results)). Day 7: send each institution its rows for comment. **Verifier and pathway.** The institutions' research offices (right of reply), CIHR, and TranspariMED as amplifier. **Headline.** "X% of trials run at Toronto's hospitals never reported results: patients enrolled, answers never shared" (Toronto Star, CBC Toronto, CMAJ news). **Main risk.** False positives and intent language; never call unreported trials "hidden", and name institutions rather than investigators. **Saturation.** Some competition on the aggregate; the current institution-level table was not found in three queries (absence-based).

### 4. Every Canadian product monograph diffed against its FDA label

**Finding.** A ranked list of drugs marketed in Canada whose US label carries a boxed warning or contraindication absent from the Canadian monograph's Serious Warnings and Precautions box, and the reverse, with side-by-side excerpts and any pending Health Canada safety review. **Already done?** Only manually and at small scale. A 2013 BMJ Quality & Safety comparison of 20 top-selling drugs found **FDA boxed warnings on 15 of 20 versus Canada's 9 of 20**, and noted that Canada has no available list of boxed-warning medicines ([BMJ Quality & Safety](https://qualitysafety.bmj.com/content/22/9/727)); Perry and Bhasale's 2020 study of 1,441 advisories from four regulators (Health Canada 370) reported a "low rate of agreement" on whether to warn at all ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32696556/); [RAPS](https://www.raps.org/resource/mhra-more-likely-than-other-regulators-to-issue-a.html)). No automated full-formulary diff surfaced. **Data and access.** The Drug Product Database extract gives DINs, ingredients and marketing status in bulk ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/drug-product-database/extracts.html)); monographs are per-product PDFs, with a running list of brand safety updates ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/drug-products/drug-product-database/label-safety-assessment-update.html)); FDA labels are structured on DailyMed (prior knowledge, unverified this round). **The week.** Day 1: list marketed human prescription ingredients and map them to FDA applications. Days 2–3: fetch each monograph PDF and SPL file; extract Boxed Warning, Contraindications and Serious Warnings sections. Days 4–5: section-by-section comparison by the agent, flagging US boxed warnings missing from the Canadian box and checking Summary Safety Reviews and InfoWatch for pending updates. Day 6: falsification, discarding differences explained by indication, formulation or Canadian synonyms. Day 7: ranked list with excerpts to Health Canada and manufacturers. **Verifier and pathway.** Health Canada's Marketed Health Products Directorate, whose monograph updates after safety reviews appear in Health Product InfoWatch ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/medeffect-canada/health-product-infowatch/april-2026.html)); the manufacturers; and the academic group already doing manual comparisons (Lexchin; Bhasale and Perry), who are both competitors and natural validators. **Headline.** "Dozens of drugs carry US black-box warnings that Canadian labels omit" (CBC, Toronto Star). **Main risk.** Low legally, because the claim is purely documentary; the ethical risk is alarming patients, so present it as label-text differences regulators may legitimately explain, not as hidden harms. **Saturation.** Open (unverified): no one was found automating the diff, in a field where the manual precedents prove the differences are real.

### 5. Municipal drinking-water reports reconciled against the ministry's own records

**Finding.** Ontario municipal systems whose O. Reg. 170/03 annual or summary reports omit or understate adverse water quality incidents, orders or convictions that appear in the ministry's open extract; systems with no findable public report; multi-year chemistry exceedances that never produced an incident; and which of these never reached local news. **Already done?** The adjacent angles are crowded: the Toronto Star's 2017 "more than 640 schools, daycares failed lead tests" ([PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/canada/toronto-star/20171006/281509341396852)), the 2019 Tainted Water consortium ([Global News](https://globalnews.ca/news/6134683/inside-the-investigation-tainted-water-canada/amp)), the IJB tracker of **more than 175,000 school and daycare results, one in ten above 5 ppb** ([IJB](https://ijb.utoronto.ca/?p=10581)), and the Auditor General's March 2025 report on non-municipal systems, where 56% of 1,660 systems missed a required sample in five years ([Auditor General of Ontario](https://auditor.on.ca/en/content/annualreports/arreports/en25/pa_drinkingwater_en25.pdf)). No 2024–2026 journalism on repeated incidents at individual municipal systems surfaced, yet the raw material is visible: Midland's 2024 report alone lists four adverse incidents including E. coli ([Midland](https://midland.ca/en/living-in-midland/resources/Water-and-Sewer-Services/Regulatory-Reporting/2024-Drinking-Water-Annual-Summary-Report.pdf)). **Data and access.** The Drinking Water Quality and Enforcement fiscal-year zips (2014-15 to at least 2023-24) with results, incidents, inspections, orders and convictions under the Open Government Licence ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data2.ontario.ca/dataset/drinking-water-quality-and-enforcement); [WCWC](https://wcwc.ca/resources/other/ontario-drinking-water-quality-and-enforcement-open-data/)); Drinking Water Surveillance Program chemistry 1998–2022 ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/drinking-water-surveillance-program)); and the municipal reports, scattered PDFs on hundreds of domains that the ministry's own form implies are not universally posted ([City of Waterloo form](https://www.waterloo.ca/media/a4ab3flj/drinking-water-annual-and-summary-report.pdf)). **The week.** Day 1: load the ministry zips and build per-system incident, order and conviction histories. Day 2: crawl municipal sites for reports and parse the incident tables. Day 3: reconcile report-stated against ministry-recorded incidents by system and year; flag mismatches and systems with no findable report. Day 4: join the surveillance chemistry (trihalomethanes, haloacetic acids, nitrate, sodium, fluoride) to find running-average exceedances that never triggered an incident. Day 5: search local outlets for each flagged system. Day 6: falsification on fiscal-versus-calendar windows, incident-versus-non-compliance definitions, and later corrective notices. Day 7: system-level evidence to the ministry and health units. **Verifier and pathway.** The Chief Drinking Water Inspector, whose 2024–25 report says 98.1% of small-system results met standards ([ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/2024-2025-chief-drinking-water-inspector-annual-report)); local medical officers of health; the Walkerton Clean Water Centre; CELA as advocate ([CELA](https://cela.ca/sign-on-letter-take-action-to-protect-drinking-water-in-non-municipal-systems/)). **Headline.** "Dozens of Ontario water systems' public reports don't match the province's own adverse-incident records" (Toronto Star, CBC). **Main risk.** Low if each finding is "report X omits incident Y" with both documents quoted; it rises sharply if framed as concealment. **Saturation.** Crowded for lead and for non-municipal systems; open niche (absence-based) for the report-versus-record reconciliation.

### 6. Toronto's Goad plans overlaid on today's schools, daycares and parks

**Finding.** Toronto schools, licensed child-care centres and parks on or within a buffer of tanneries, gas works, foundries, lead, paint and dye works and coal yards recorded on the 1880–1924 Goad fire-insurance plans, where no public Record of Site Condition or City environmental assessment can be found, reported publicly at neighbourhood level and privately at parcel level. **Already done?** Not systematically. Boston overlaid Sanborn industrial uses on health data in 2004 ([Esri ArcNews](https://esri.com/news/arcnews/summer03articles/boston-mass.html)) and New York built a historic land-use dataset from Sanborn maps ([data.gov](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/historic-land-use-data)), but no study linking fire-insurance maps to school sites was found. Toronto's precedents are site by site: lead up to nine times provincial limits 20 cm down at Swansea Public School, attributed to 1940s industrial landfill ([Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/news/national/board-wants-lead-tests-of-soil-at-swansea-school/article4150966)); South Riverdale's **1988 $10 million lead-soil replacement at about 900 properties** ([Toronto Public Library](https://digitalarchive.tpl.ca/objects/372696/getting-lead-out)); and a 1998 City-backed guide to building exactly such an inventory ([CCLM Portal](https://www.cclmportal.ca/resource/historical-land-use-inventories-guide-their-creation)). **Data and access.** U of T's Map and Data Library has digitized and georeferenced every out-of-copyright fire-insurance plan and atlas plate of Toronto and makes them downloadable ([U of T MDL](https://mdl-data.library.utoronto.ca/items/show/1813)); licensed child-care facilities are open and updated monthly ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/en/dataset/licensed-child-care-facilities-in-ontario)). The bottleneck is the other side: Records of Site Condition are per-site documents with no bulk download found, sold per site by ERIS ([ERIS](https://www.erisinfo.com/products/ontario-rsc/)), and the provincial contaminated-sites database is "under review" and unavailable ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/contaminated-sites-databasehistorical-landfills-database)). **The week.** Day 1: download the Goad sheets and build a gazetteer of hazardous-use labels. Days 2–3: OCR and vision pass over a few hundred plates, extract labelled polygons, dedupe across editions, geocode. Day 4: overlay on schools, child-care centres and parks; compute buffer hits. Day 5: cross-check each hit against the site-condition registry by address, City assessment PDFs and the 1998 landfill list. Day 6: falsification, removing sites remediated or redeveloped under a Record of Site Condition and down-weighting small or short-lived uses. Day 7: a neighbourhood-level map and a confidential parcel list. **Verifier and pathway.** TDSB and TCDSB facilities staff, Toronto Public Health, the Ministry of the Environment, and the Phase 1 consultants who do this per site for a fee. **Headline.** "Dozens of Toronto schools and daycares sit on or beside century-old tanneries, gas works and foundries, and there's no public record they were ever tested" (Toronto Star, The Varsity). **Main risk.** The highest on this list. Perceived risk alone lowers property values ([Sustainability](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/5/2046)), although Ontario's Court of Appeal held in Tridan v. Shell that knowing a site was once polluted is not in itself stigma after a clean-up ([Dalhousie Journal of Legal Studies](https://digitalcommons.schulichlaw.dal.ca/djls/vol11/iss1/6)); the publishable claim is only "historic use plus no public record of assessment", never contamination, and never a parcel. **Saturation.** Open niche (absence-based) for the systematic overlay; some competition from consultants.

### 7. Retracted evidence inside Canadian clinical guidelines

**Finding.** Which current Canadian guidelines (Task Force on Preventive Health Care, Canadian Cardiovascular Society, Diabetes Canada, Ontario Health and Cancer Care Ontario, Health Canada and PHAC guidance, CMAJ guidelines, Choosing Wisely Canada) cite retracted papers, which cited them after the retraction date without acknowledgement, and whether any recommendation rests on one. **Already done?** Globally, yes; for Canada, no audit was found in four queries. At the 2025 Peer Review Congress, Besançon, Cabanac and Weber-Boer reported **398 guideline–retracted-publication pairs cited after retraction, none acknowledging the retracted status** ([Peer Review Congress](https://peerreviewcongress.org/abstract/retracted-publications-referenced-in-clinical-guidelines/)); the VITALITY I study in The BMJ found that removing retracted trials changed 218 meta-analyses and touched **157 guideline documents** ([BMJ via UEA](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/99702/1/bmj-2024-082068.pdf); [AusDoc](https://www.ausdoc.com.au/news/157-clinical-practice-guidelines-contaminated-by-retracted-studies-say-researchers/)), and VITALITY II will test recommendation-level credibility ([BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/16/3/e115303)). Kataoka found only 11% of affected guidelines ever corrected themselves ([medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.01.30.22270124)). The only Canadian precedent is the Star's coverage of the Motherisk column retractions ([Toronto Star via Hypertension Canada](https://hypertension.ca/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/We-did-the-right-thing_-Medical-journals-are-retracting-columns-on-a-Toronto-newborns-death-after-reviews-conclude-the-findings-were-unreliable-_-The-Star.pdf)). **Data and access.** The Retraction Watch database is a Crossref-hosted CSV updated every working day ([Crossref](https://crossref.org/documentation/retrieve-metadata/retraction-watch/)); OpenAlex's `is_retracted` flag over-included corrections for a period and is only a first filter ([Hauschke and Nazarovets](https://elibrary.kubg.edu.ua/id/eprint/51506/)); guideline reference lists must be parsed from PDFs. **The week.** Day 1: clone the retractions CSV and index by DOI, PMID and title. Days 1–2: enumerate the Canadian corpus (CMAJ and Task Force documents are open access) and extract references with fuzzy DOI matching. Days 2–3: match and record retraction date against guideline date. Days 3–5: for each hit, have the agent locate the recommendation that cites it, whether it is the sole supporting evidence and whether it is current; re-run any tabulated meta-analysis with the trial removed. Days 5–7: falsification (confirm status on the journal site, check for guideline updates, check whether the citation is to a later-retracted review rather than the trial) and a per-recommendation evidence table. **Verifier and pathway.** The guideline body, CMAJ's editors and Retraction Watch; Prescrire has already noted that developers "do not always run checks for retracted articles" ([Prescrire](https://english.prescrire.org/EN/81/168/69497/0/NewsDetails.aspx?page=5)). **Headline.** "Canadian clinical guidelines still cite retracted studies, and nobody flagged it" (CMAJ news, Retraction Watch); a load-bearing case reaches CBC or the Star. **Main risk.** The headline degrades to background citations if no recommendation depends on a retracted trial. Legally, naming a guideline and a retracted paper is safe because retraction is a public fact; sleuths who characterised authors have been sued ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/litigation/sarkar-v-does/)), and Ontario's anti-SLAPP provision helps without stopping a demand letter ([Law Times](https://www.lawtimesnews.com/practice-areas/litigation/covid-vaccine-skeptic-doctor-loses-anti-slapp-case-at-court-of-appeal/384083)). **Saturation.** Open niche inside a crowded field, absence-based.

### 8. Dead cross-references in Ontario's and Canada's statute books

**Finding.** Every reference in Ontario's consolidated statutes and the federal consolidated Acts and regulations that points to a repealed Act, a non-existent section, a duplicated number or a definition never used, classified into consolidation errors the legislative counsel can fix, drafting anomalies for the federal miscellaneous-amendment program, and dead letters. **Already done?** No Canadian scan was found. The closest work is a Luxembourg group's automated cross-reference resolver, validated on Ontario's Personal Health Information Protection Act with reported precision of 99.7% for identification and 99.9% for resolution ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6912248/); [Requirements Engineering](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00766-015-0241-3)); the federal Statutes Repeal Act already polices unproclaimed provisions through an annual report ([Justice Laws](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/Reports/2026.html)), and the Miscellaneous Statute Law Amendment Program accepts proposals from "anyone" ([Senate of Canada](https://sencanada.ca/en/content/sen/committee/441/lcjc/74ev-56393-e)). **Data and access.** Federal consolidated Acts and regulations as official bulk XML updated every two weeks, with point-in-time versions back to 2003 ([Open Government](https://donnees.iriu.ca/en/dataset/e79145a7-8ab7-4582-b5df-f357d3a9dd0c)), official for evidentiary purposes since 2009 ([Justice Laws](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/faq/)); Ontario has no confirmed official XML, but a 5,096-document community scrape under an MIT licence exists ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/hordruma/ontario_laws_and_regs)). **The week.** Day 1: download both corpora. Day 2: parse section, subsection, definition and Act references with regex plus the agent for ambiguous cases. Day 3: resolve each against its target and flag failures. Day 4: falsification against point-in-time XML (was the target renumbered?), the Table of Public Statutes and Ontario's legislative tables, discarding transitional provisions. Day 5: classify survivors. Day 6: draft submissions to Ontario's Office of Legislative Counsel and the federal Justice Legislation Section. Day 7: write-up. This is the most deterministic loop on the list. **Verifier and pathway.** Ontario's Chief Legislative Counsel must correct consolidation errors under the Legislation Act, 2006 and publishes correction and change notices ([CIAJ slides](https://ciaj-icaj.ca/wp-content/uploads/documents/2018/11/tamara-kuzyk-ontarios-change-powers-ppt.pdf?id=9840); [ontario.ca](https://ontario.ca/laws/consolidated-statutes-correction-notices)), so action is publicly visible. **Headline.** "Student's AI sweep finds dozens of dead references in Ontario's law books; province issues corrections" (The Varsity, Canadian Lawyer, Law Times); mainstream pickup needs a dangling reference that affects rights, such as a penalty clause pointing at a repealed section. **Main risk.** Minimal; no individuals are named. The e-Laws terms of use for scraping are unverified. **Saturation.** Open niche (unverified).

**Where to start.** Spend day one on the re-checks in the final section and on two emails: one to the Petroleum Operations Section asking whether the ministry's post-Wheatley "new technology" mapping already uses historical maps or LiDAR, and one to the Accessibility Directorate asking whether any province-wide scan exists. If the ministry says nobody is reading the old maps, commit the week to the wells, with the water-well gas mining as the second evidence layer: it is the only idea here whose finding is both genuinely new and tied to a hazard that has already injured people. If the reply is "we are already doing it", run the accessibility sweep instead, because it is the loop most certain to end the week with a publishable, checkable table and the lowest legal exposure.

## Running the loop so the result survives a verifier and a lawyer

The precedents from the first round of this research all follow one shape, amateur detection then professional confirmation, and the impact projects above add a second: institutions, not individuals, as subjects. Five practices follow.

**Prove it is not already known before the loop starts.** The dominant failure in AI-assisted discovery is rediscovery; OpenAI's October 2025 Erdős claims were refuted within hours because the model had found existing papers ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45633482)), and the one confirmed amateur win of 2026 began with a search across 36 catalogues and 340,505 automated alerts ([Rabtsevich gist](https://gist.github.com/MrBoostie/b7a34b9ec96fbfb37e8fd250ba6debba)). Each card above names its equivalent, from the ministry's answer on well mapping to the VITALITY dataset. Keep a dated provenance log, because the 2026 Anthropic–Copenhagen enzyme dispute showed that "who knew what, when" becomes the story when priority is contested ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/28/1145230/when-can-we-say-ai-made-a-scientific-discovery/)).

**Schedule the falsification pass, with a second agent as adversary.** Every card reserves day six for trying to destroy the result. The reproducibility literature warns that prompt wording can push an agent into confirmatory specification search ([arXiv 2606.11447](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.11447)), so a fresh session told only to find the flaw, ideally on a different vendor's model, reviews the write-up first; the exoplanet case credited this pattern with removing "at least one overstrong statistical claim" ([Rabtsevich gist](https://gist.github.com/MrBoostie/b7a34b9ec96fbfb37e8fd250ba6debba)). Report error bands: the trials table is credible only with a hand-checked miss rate, because the 2016 institution-level numbers were rebutted for lacking one ([CAREB](https://careb-accer.org/cbc-news-article-re-results-missing-from-clinical-trials-registered-by-canadian-universities-and-research-hospitals/)).

**Preregister the method before the data is touched.** Freeze the decision rules on day one (the 100 m distance rule, the WCAG 2.0 AA rule set, the two-year completion window, the definition of a mismatch) in a timestamped public record with code checksums, and keep the frozen version separate from exploratory variants. Preregistration converts "a student found some numbers" into a checkable claim and is the strongest defence against the accusation of fishing.

**Disclose to the verifier before any publication, in the verifier's format.** Each card names the office that can act. Send the full evidence privately, give a fixed period for comment, and let the institutional response (a field check, a correction notice, a monograph update) become the hook the press covers. This is what the Supreme Court's responsible-communication defence rewards: diligence proportional to the seriousness of the claim and an attempt to get the subject's side, available to any communicator, not only journalists ([Osgoode TheCourt](https://www.yorku.ca/osgoode/thecourt/2010/01/11/grant-v-torstar-corp-responsible-communication-on-matters-of-public-interest/)).

**Name institutions, never individuals; aggregate anything that touches a property.** Ontario's anti-SLAPP provision has dismissed suits against public-interest critics, including a $2 million claim by a pharmaceutical company in November 2025 ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/canada/libel-defamation/1750648/online-criticism-and-defamation-an-ontario-anti-slapp-decision)), but a demand letter still arrives first. The safe forms are documentary: "report X omits incident Y", "the label says A, the monograph says B", "this guideline cites DOI Z, retracted on date D", never "concealed", "hidden" or "negligent". Research-integrity flags go to authors first, then editors, then PubPeer as checkable facts without intent claims. For the wells and the Goad overlay, parcel-level results go only to the ministry and the school boards; public outputs are neighbourhood maps and counts, because landowners inherit plugging liability ([ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/abandoned-works-program)) and perceived contamination risk alone moves property values ([Sustainability](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/12/5/2046)). The loop itself must stay lawful: respect robots.txt and rate limits, never run a continuous crawl against a site whose terms bar automated collection, and treat every scraped page as data, not instruction.

## Conclusion

The shift from the first round of this research to this one is a shift in what "discovery" means: not an object in a registry but a discrepancy between what an institution says and what its own records show, found by an agent that can read ten thousand PDFs in a week. That changes the risk profile. The astronomy and mathematics projects could only embarrass the student; these can harm a landowner, a school's reputation or a hospital's standing if a false positive leaks, which is why the ranking rewards projects whose output is a list for an official to check (wells, label gaps, dead statute references) over projects whose output is an accusation (donors, chatbots, methane). The pipeline is the cheap part; the scarce inputs are the day spent proving nobody has done it, the frozen method, and the relationship with the one office that can turn a candidate list into a field visit, a correction notice or a monograph update. A student who sends the Petroleum Operations Section a hundred well candidates with evidence cards, and the Accessibility Directorate a reproducible league table, will have done something no funded group in Ontario has done, and the headline will follow the ministry's reply rather than the AI.
