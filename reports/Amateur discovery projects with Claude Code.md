# Hunt the gaps automated pipelines leave behind

The amateur-plus-AI-agent discoveries of 2025–26 share one structure — a public archive or open-problem list with a known blind spot, an agent-driven search-then-falsify loop, and a professional gatekeeper who confirms — and that structure is now claimed in four niches: shallow short-period TESS planet candidates, "easy" Erdős proofs, the Cryptiana list of historical ciphers, and LLM sweeps of OEIS conjectures and of the published literature for arithmetic errors. Everything else is open to a greater or lesser degree. For a Toronto mathematics undergraduate with a laptop, free data and no supervisor, the best-evidenced bets are **a new minor meteor shower or parent-body link mined from the Western University–led Global Meteor Network's public orbits**, **archival precovery of just-announced near-Earth objects and comets in public survey images**, and **a tested pipeline ready for Gaia DR4 on 2 December 2026**; locally, **an Ontario quarry-blast catalogue from open seismic data** and **a LiDAR survey of GTA ravines for undocumented 19th-century features** are open niches with named official verifiers. The pattern across every verified case is "amateur detection, professional confirmation": no AI transcript was ever accepted as evidence, and the two fatal errors — rediscovering something already published, and trusting one agent's statistics — were avoided only by exhaustive prior-claim search and cross-vendor adversarial review. Budget as much effort for the literature check and the gatekeeper hand-off as for the search itself, and treat the dozen or so "open niche" ratings below that rest on absence of search evidence as hypotheses to re-check in a day before committing a term.

## Every verified amateur-plus-AI win so far ran through a gatekeeper

The case that inspired this question is narrower than its headlines. Pavel Rabtsevich, a 28-year-old product manager in Spain with no astronomy training and no telescope, used Claude Code for "data downloading and parsing, transit-search code, fitting, nearby-star checks, false-positive checks, plotting, and reruns", with OpenAI Codex and separate clean agent sessions as independent reviewers, to flag **TIC 4206066** — a late-K dwarf about 116 light-years away — as hosting a **3.18-day, ~1.4-Earth-radius candidate** at 0.04–0.05 % depth, first seen in TESS Sector 98 and traced back to Sectors 32 and 6 ([GitHub gist](https://gist.github.com/MrBoostie/b7a34b9ec96fbfb37e8fd250ba6debba)). Press accounts put the effort at **about two weeks, 74 analyses and more than 1,000 scripts** ([EGamers.io](https://egamers.io/amateur-uses-claude-code-codex-and-nasa-data-to-flag-a-possible-exoplanet-and-tess-will-test-it-in-november/)). What made it credible was conventional: a search across "36 catalogs/literature sources and 340,505 automated TESS alerts", TRICERATOPS false-positive probabilities of ~0.03–0.04, Gaia vetting, pixel-level localisation, and — decisively — preregistered predictions (nine transit times, expected depths, frozen code with checksums) published before **TESS DDT Program 100 re-observes the star at 2-minute cadence from 31 October to 26 November 2026** ([GitHub gist](https://gist.github.com/MrBoostie/b7a34b9ec96fbfb37e8fd250ba6debba); [TESS DDT list](https://tess.mit.edu/science/ddt/)). The author's own words are the ones to remember: "this is not statistically validated and not confirmed as a planet", and "an audit removed at least one overstrong statistical claim". The DDT approval is a decision to test, not a confirmation; the outcome arrives after 26 November 2026. Scientific American reports Yale's Greg Laughlin calling the case for at least one planet "solid" ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/amateur-astronomer-harnesses-ai-to-find-a-possible-hidden-exoplanet/)), and the case has already spawned third-party replication repositories ([xaviercallens/TIC4206066-Exoplanet-Verification](https://github.com/xaviercallens/TIC4206066-Exoplanet-Verification)), while professional uniform-vetting catalogues such as COUNTESS now cover the same parameter space ([arXiv 2606.13789](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13789)). A second Claude-Code-finds-a-small-TESS-planet story would not be news unless the object were exceptional.

Mathematics is the other field where true amateurs have been credited. Liam Price, 23, "has no advanced mathematics training" and said "I don't even know what this problem is"; a single prompt to GPT-5.4 Pro produced the approach that solved **Erdős Problem #1196** on primitive sets, which Stanford's Jared Lichtman called "rough" and which became an eight-author arXiv paper with Terence Tao, noting the method "seems to have been overlooked by the prior literature since Erdős' seminal 1935 paper" ([Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/amateur-armed-with-chatgpt-vibe-maths-a-60-year-old-problem); [heise](https://www.heise.de/en/news/Creative-solution-AI-solves-60-year-old-Erd-s-problem-11276442.html); [arXiv 2605.00301](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.00301); [erdosproblems.com/1196](https://www.erdosproblems.com/1196)). This sits inside a much larger sweep: AI tools helped move **about 100 Erdős problems to "solved" between October 2025 and February 2026**, mostly by "souped-up literature search" ([Scientific American PDF](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~williams/scientificamerican2.pdf)); a DeepMind paper counts **483 of 1,179 problems (41 %) solved** by February 2026 ([arXiv 2601.22401](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22401v2)); GPT-5.2 Pro proofs of #397, #728 and #729 were driven by a software engineer and a Cambridge undergraduate in January 2026 ([delante](https://delante.co/gpt-5-2-solves-erdos-problems/); [arXiv 2601.07421](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.07421)); AlphaProof Nexus proved **9 of 353 open Erdős problems and 44 of 492 open OEIS conjectures** for "a few hundred dollars per problem" ([the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/google-deepminds-alphaproof-nexus-solves-decades-old-math-problems-for-a-few-hundred-dollars/)); and an internal OpenAI model produced the counterexample to the 1946 unit-distance conjecture in May 2026 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/05/20/openai-claims-it-solved-an-80-year-old-math-problem-for-real-this-time/)). Tao's calibration is sobering: the early solves were "lowest hanging fruit", he estimates **only 1–2 % of open Erdős problems are currently accessible to AI with minimal human help**, and a database of attempts shows success rates "of just one to two percent, clustered around easier problems" ([the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/terence-tao-says-gpt-5-2-pro-cracked-an-erdos-problem-but-warns-the-win-says-more-about-speed-than-difficulty/); [the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/gpt-5-2-pro-solves-another-erdos-problem-while-a-new-database-reveals-most-attempts-still-fail/)). Thomas Bloom nonetheless told Quanta that "most of the new results had come from hobbyists and undergraduates using publicly available LLMs" ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-the-legendary-erdos-problems-are-falling-to-ai-20260803/)), and a separate credit model now exists for individuals who supply computational certificates rather than proofs: the Problem 647 paper credits "Jamal Agbanwa's AI-assisted possible solution, bentrd's frontier extension, and Patrik Idén's computational verification up to 10^12", and two other individuals "published a kernel-checked verification of a negative resolution of Problem 486" ([arXiv 2608.17880](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17880)).

Historical ciphers were swept in roughly six weeks. Carter Church, a 27-year-old AI engineer, used an OpenAI model to decrypt an 1809 Napoleon–Marmont letter "unread for 217 years" in about six hours, working from a 1969 reproduction and a partial key catalogued by historian Daniel Tant; he said the AI "mostly contributed persistence" ([Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/02/ai-decodes-217-year-old-napoleonic-letter-in-6-hours)). The validator was Satoshi Tomokiyo, who runs the Cryptiana list of unsolved ciphers and told AFP that **"most ciphers on his list had been cracked with AI since mid-September"** 2026, with others using "Anthropic's top models for similar work" ([AFP via eNCA](https://www.enca.com/lifestyle/mixed-feelings-ai-cracks-centuries-old-ciphers)). On OEIS, an insurance worker who "solve[s] puzzles of all kinds for fun" had AI-assisted edits to two entries approved and flagged "eight recorded formulas that appear to fail on a published term" ([SeqFan](https://groups.google.com/g/seqfan/c/NZBtLoJFm1c)); DeepMind has formalised 492 open OEIS conjectures in Lean, and the best model in a related benchmark "resolved 147 of the 492" while "one claimed disproof was wrong" ([SeqFan](https://groups.google.com/g/seqfan/c/gbb7MKvandc)). Hobbyists have used Claude Code to reverse-engineer legacy binary formats in an afternoon ([Reddit snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1r05l40/snapshots/2026-02-09T18%3A17%3A20.101409Z)), but such work earns Reddit threads, not newspaper coverage, because no external authority confirms it. LLM error-hunting across the published literature is industrialised: YesNoError scanned more than 37,000 papers in about two months and the Black Spatula Project had reviewed ~500 with ~10 % false positives ([heise](https://www.heise.de/en/news/No-more-calculation-errors-AI-checks-papers-for-errors-Why-that-s-not-enough-10321469.html)); a GPT-5-based checker reached 83.2 % precision on AI papers ([arXiv 2512.05925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05925)). Corporate claims are a different category and often conflated: Anthropic's 950-agent enzyme "discovery" of September 2026 ran straight into a priority dispute with a Copenhagen group that had fed unpublished data into Claude ([ThePrint](https://theprint.in/science/did-claude-discover-a-new-enzyme-or-stole-a-biologists-work-anthropic-responds/3057122/); [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/28/1145230/when-can-we-say-ai-made-a-scientific-discovery/)).

The failure modes are as instructive as the wins. OpenAI's October 2025 claim that GPT-5 solved ten Erdős problems "was publicly refuted within hours" because the model had found existing papers; Bloom explained that "open" on his site "simply meant he was unaware of published solutions" ([Gary Marcus](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/erdosgate); [Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45633482)). An LLM "solution" to the 1653 Urquhart cipher claimed in August 2026 drew a GitHub rebuttal within a day ([pasqualepillitteri.it, secondary and unverified](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/de/news/16170/claude-fable-5-1-knackt-urquhart-chiffre)). A 2025–26 X post about "10 more planets" had "no preprint, data release, or NASA record behind it" and was treated as a claim ([ExplainX](https://explainx.ai/blog/claude-code-planet-candidate-tic-4206066-tess-rabtsevich-2026)). Tao's point that "negative results rarely get published, while positive results go viral" means the visible base rate is inflated. And in every amateur case the professionals entered before the finding counted: Tao and Lichtman co-authored, Tomokiyo adjudicated, OEIS editors approved, the TESS committee allocated time. No case was found of an amateur whose finding was confirmed without a professional intermediary.

## Fifteen ideas survive the novelty, doability and verifiability test

The shortlist below ranks on three things in order: novelty (is there evidence nobody has done this, and how strong is that evidence), doability (laptop, free data, weeks to a few months, no supervisor), and verifiability (is there a named gatekeeper who will stamp the result and thereby make it newsworthy). Newsworthiness and a Toronto/Ontario angle break ties. Saturation uses four grades. **Crowded** means several active groups or a published AI sweep already occupy the exact parameter space. **Some competition** means a small circle does this and still finds things every year, with room for a careful newcomer. **Open niche** means the workflow is proven elsewhere and no attempt was found for this target or region; this grade rests partly on absence of search evidence. **Open (unverified)** means no one was found doing it but the specific confirmatory searches could not be run, so the rating is a hypothesis. The time estimates are the researchers' judgement, not sourced figures.

### 1. A new meteor shower or parent body from Global Meteor Network orbits

**Discovery.** A previously unlisted minor shower (which receives an "M2026-xx" provisional designation), confirmation of a 2024 or 2025 shower's return, or identification of the parent asteroid or comet for one of the showers found last year. **Already done?** Some competition, and the field is visibly still producing: GMN's 2025 report lists **418 showers identified cumulatively** and roughly eight new ones in 2025 alone — M2025-S1 (Delphinus), S2 (Hydrus), U1 (Pegasus), V1 (Monoceros), Y1 (a strong mid-December outburst of 370 meteors), plus showers in Eridanus and Aries — while M2024-S1 and M2024-N1 were confirmed on their 2025 returns ([eMeteorNews](https://www.emeteornews.net/2026/02/06/global-meteor-network-report-2025/); [eMeteorNews](https://www.emeteornews.net/2025/10/28/m2024-s1-activity-confirmed-in-2025/)). Most reports come from the GMN core team and a few European amateurs; a researcher has described finding a new shower whose parent is "an asteroid getting broken down by the Sun" ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/i-found-a-new-meteor-shower-and-it-comes-from-an-asteroid-getting-broken-down-by-the-sun-277557)). Whether the 2025 showers already have parent-body identifications was not verified. **Data and method.** GMN is led by Denis Vida at Western University, with more than 450 cameras in 23 countries including Ontario ([Western News](https://news.westernu.ca/2021/07/global-meteor-network/); [MNRAS](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/506/4/5046/6347233)); load a season's orbits, cluster in orbital-element or radiant–velocity space using the D-criteria of the March 2026 method paper ([eMeteorNews](https://www.emeteornews.net/2026/03/20/meteoroid-orbit-shower-identification-method-and-its-application-in-meteor-shower-case-studies/)), exclude known IAU MDC showers, test against the sporadic background; for parents, compute D-criterion similarity to all numbered asteroids and comets and integrate orbits. **Verification.** Report to the IAU Meteor Data Center for a provisional designation and publish in eMeteorNews or WGN; the GMN team co-authors with amateurs and is two hours away. **Main risk.** Random clustering of sporadics and small-number statistics, or duplicating an existing MDC entry under a new name. The GMN data URL and CC-BY licence come from prior knowledge and must be confirmed first.

### 2. Archival precovery of just-announced asteroids and comets

**Discovery.** Pre-discovery detections of a newly announced object in public survey images, extending its orbital arc by years; this can flip an impact probability, confirm a resonant orbit, or find an interstellar object's early trail. **Already done?** Some competition and the highest news value per find of anything on this list. California amateur Sam Deen found 2016 pre-discovery images of asteroid **2024 YR4** that ruled out a lunar impact to ~22,000 km, matching JWST ([Sky & Telescope](https://skyandtelescope.org/astronomy-news/lunar-impact-from-asteroid-2024-yr4-ruled-out/)); he found ATLAS pre-discovery observations of interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3I/ATLAS)) and 2018 images of C/2025 D1, earning co-authorship ([Astronomy.com](https://www.astronomy.com/science/astronomers-have-spotted-the-most-distant-comet-ever-discovered/)). No AI-assisted amateur doing this was found, and professionals reprocess whole surveys only occasionally. **Data and method.** Take targets from the MPC's NEO Confirmation Page and MPECs; compute the ephemeris and its uncertainty back 5–15 years; query image archives (IRSA for ZTF/PTF, NOIRLab's archive for DECam, Pan-STARRS cutouts, and the Canadian Astronomy Data Centre's solar-system image search — all archive URLs are from prior knowledge, unverified) for frames covering the uncertainty ellipse; detect faint moving sources; measure astrometry against Gaia; submit multi-position tracklets. **Verification.** The MPC credits the measurer by name in each submission ([MPC](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/iau/info/ObsDetails.html)), and precoveries appear in MPECs. **Main risk.** Since July 2025 everyone submitting observations from public archives must pass a verification process run by the Singletons and Archival Submission Committee ([MPC Newsletter, July 2025](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/media/newsletters/MPC_Newsletter_Jul2025.pdf); [October 2025](https://minorplanetcenter.net/media/newsletters/MPC_Newsletter_Oct2025.pdf)); single positions cause a whole submission to be rejected ([MPC guide](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/iau/info/Astrometry.html)); and mistaking artefacts or known asteroids for the target is the classic error. Plan for an onboarding hurdle before the first newsworthy object appears.

### 3. A Gaia DR4 pipeline ready on 2 December 2026

**Discovery.** Eclipsing binaries or periodic variables in DR4 epoch photometry not yet in VSX, or sub-stellar astrometric companions around nearby stars, found in the first weeks after release. **Already done?** Open for weeks, then crowded. No amateur-credited Gaia discovery was found for 2023–26 because DR3 binaries and white dwarfs are professional territory (tens of thousands of candidates per paper), but **Gaia DR4, the first "principal" release covering 5.5 years, is scheduled for Wednesday 2 December 2026**, with a pre-release of astrometric time series issued on 29 June 2026 for testing ([ESA Gaia news](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/news-2026)); the NASA Exoplanet Archive expects DR4 to bring the first large set of astrometric exoplanet candidates ([Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/docs/GaiaMission.html)). Professional catalogues will land within months, so the window is the first weeks. **Data and method.** Build and test the pipeline now on DR3 plus the pre-release: period searches on epoch photometry for a sky region, cross-match against VSX (a Hugging Face mirror exists for offline matching), Gaia's own variability tables and ZTF; or filter DR4 astrometric binary solutions for sub-stellar companions. **Verification.** VSX entries carry discoverer credit and moderators coach first-timers ([AAVSO forum](https://forums.aavso.org/t/how-to-vsx-and-variable-star-discovery/1490)); an RNAAS note for the astrometric route. **Main risk.** Gaia scanning-law aliases in periodograms, and duplicating DR4's own classification tables — check those first on release day. This is the one idea with a hard external deadline; everything else can slip.

### 4. An Ontario quarry-blast catalogue from open seismic data

**Discovery.** Recurring blast sources near Toronto, their weekday and time-of-day patterns, blasts missing from the public catalogue, and any catalogued "earthquakes" that are actually blasts. **Already done?** Open niche. Canada's National Earthquake Database paper states that in eastern Canada "blast events in this region may outnumber earthquakes by approximately 5:1" and describes the public blast catalogue as analyst-reviewed but incomplete ([Seismica](https://seismica.library.mcgill.ca/article/download/1964/3719/34421)). The precedent for the headline exists: a December 2019 M2.1 "earthquake" near Wiarton was traced to a quarry, and NRCan's analyst explained that blasts start sharply and fade quickly whereas earthquakes build ([CTV London](https://london.ctvnews.ca/that-earthquake-in-wiarton-ont-wasn-t-really-an-earthquake-1.4736929)). The nearest analogues are a Raspberry Shake owner in Montreal who recorded a limestone-quarry blast logged as M2.1 ([Raspberry Shake community](https://community.raspberryshake.org/t/montreal-urban-seismology-on-a-weekday/3110)) and a 2024 study that located and classified construction blasts in Oslo with up to 11 Raspberry Shakes ([Seismica via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/5329104a5d3442a7be797e11a0608300)); nothing for Ontario. **Data and method.** Earthquakes Canada event lists and FDSN waveforms from the CN network, plus citizen Raspberry Shake stations served as network RASPISHAKE ([FDSN](https://www.fdsn.org/datacenters/detail/RASPISHAKE/)); an ObsPy pipeline with STA/LTA detection, a blast-versus-earthquake classifier on onset sharpness, duration, spectral ratio and daytime-weekday occurrence, arrival-time location, and clustering to the escarpment quarries around Milton, Acton and Flamborough, Bruce County and eastern Ontario. **Verification.** NRCan's analysts already review blasts and have discussed them publicly; the database authors acknowledge the incompleteness. **Main risk.** The number of Raspberry Shake stations in Ontario was not verified, and a blast-pattern story is modest news unless a misclassified event turns up — which is exactly what the pipeline should be built to find.

### 5. A LiDAR survey of Toronto's ravines for lost 19th-century landscape features

**Discovery.** Specific, previously unrecorded historic-era features — mill races and dams, lime kilns, relict road alignments, farmstead foundations, field boundaries, logging and charcoal features — in the Humber, Don, Rouge, Credit and Duffins corridors and the Oak Ridges Moraine, each shown to be absent from modern records. **Already done?** Open niche in Ontario: five searches found no amateur or academic LiDAR site-detection work for the province, while the same workflow has produced headline amateur finds elsewhere. A retired road engineer used open Environment Agency LiDAR to find a 23-mile Roman road he had sought for 45 years ([Quartz](https://qz.com/616725/amateur-archaeologists-are-finding-lost-roman-roads-using-lasers)); an Exeter lockdown project with volunteers found "more than 20 miles of Roman road, 30 prehistoric or Roman settlements, and 20 prehistoric burial mounds" ([Smithsonian](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/amateur-archaeologists-make-discoveries-during-lockdown-180974886/)); the Dutch Heritage Quest project mapped 2,000 km² in three months at ~90 % accuracy with human verification ([Lidar News](https://lidarnews.com/citizens-lidar-archaeological-discoveries-in-the-netherlands/)). **Data and method.** The Ontario Digital Terrain Model (lidar-derived) comes as 1 km tiles under the Open Government Licence with a public tile index ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data.ontario.ca/dataset/ontario-digital-terrain-model-lidar-derived); [tile index](https://www.publicdocs.mnr.gov.on.ca/mirb/OntarioDTM_LidarDerived_TileIndex.zip)); classified point clouds are also open ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data2.ontario.ca/dataset/ontario-classified-point-cloud-lidar-derived)); NRCan's HRDEM adds 1–2 m DTMs with more than 709,000 km² added since May 2024 ([NRCan](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/science-data/science-research/geomatics/more-high-resolution-lidar-elevation-data-now-available)). Compute multi-directional hillshade, local relief and sky-view factor; optionally retrain the open ADAF detector ([JAS: Reports](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352409X26001689)) or use the Kisatchie trick of simulated training examples from a dozen known sites ([Archaeology Magazine](https://archaeologymag.com/2026/08/ai-trained-on-simulated-sites-finds-unknown-features/)); cross-reference Tremaine 1860, county atlases, Goad plans and the 1947 aerials; eliminate pipelines and modern grading — one UK amateur's "Roman road" was a gas main ([Kent Archaeological Society](https://www.kentarchaeology.org.uk/magazine/126/07-lidar-landscape-investigations)). **Verification.** Send a private dossier to archaeology@ontario.ca, which invites reports ([ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/archaeology)), and approach the Ontario Archaeological Society's Toronto chapter or TRCA heritage staff for a licensed walkover. **Main risk.** Legal and ethical. Ontario's site database "is not and will not be made available" ([Ontario Data Catalogue](https://data2.ontario.ca/dataset/ontario-archaeological-sites)); any fieldwork needs a licence under Regulation 881 ([Ontario e-Laws](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/900881)); a "look, don't touch" surface visit is fine, digging or collecting is not. Publish only historic-industrial features; never coordinates of a suspected precontact or burial site. The Toronto acquisition year of the provincial DTM is unverified — the tile index is the first download.

### 6. A kernel-checked computational certificate on a "tractable" Erdős problem

**Discovery.** A verified finite-case resolution, counterexample, or verification bound (for example "holds up to 10^12") on a still-open Erdős problem, wrapped in a Lean-kernel-checked certificate. **Already done?** Crowded for proof-style "easy" problems — labs and GPT-5.2 Pro hobbyists are harvesting those — but only some competition for computational certificates, where a handful of named individuals have been credited so far ([arXiv 2608.17880](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.17880)). This is the lane that fits an undergraduate who has done MAT301 and MAT315: the credit model already exists, the verification is mechanical, and Bloom records contributions on the site. **Data and method.** erdosproblems.com forum pages carry community markers "This problem looks tractable" and whether someone is working on it ([forum thread 710](https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/710)); a public Claude Code skill already queries "problems marked tractable by community" ([erdos-solver skill](https://sharedcontext.ai/skills/external/0bserver07/erdos-solver)), which also tells you others are looking. Choose a problem whose statement has a finite checkable instance; literature-check first; write a fast search in PARI/GP or Python; wrap the result in Lean as in the Problem 647 paper. Pair it with a new OEIS sequence for the extremal function the problem asks about — Erdős-linked OEIS sequences are an open sub-niche even though OEIS itself caps new submitters at three pending entries because editors are "drowning in submissions" ([OEIS wiki](https://oeis.org/wiki/Why_am_I_limited_to_3_submissions)). **Verification.** Post the literature check on the problem's forum thread before starting; Bloom verifies and credits; an arXiv note. Quanta profiled hobbyist and undergraduate solvers, so a credited entry is the most "paper-covers-it" outcome in mathematics ([Quanta](https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-the-legendary-erdos-problems-are-falling-to-ai-20260803/)). **Main risk.** The problem is already solved in literature the site has not caught — "it can be very challenging to determine for sure whether a particular problem is actually open" ([arXiv 2511.16072](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.16072)) — or the finite verification is judged trivial. A certificate is a contribution, not a solution; frame it that way.

### 7. Mining Ontario's iNaturalist backlog for first provincial and national records

**Discovery.** Species with Ontario observations absent from provincial or national checklists, confirmed to research grade and exported to GBIF — in under-identified taxa such as moths, flies, spiders, lichens, fungi, crayfish, and singing insects found in audio. **Already done?** Some competition for posting observations, which is crowded, but the systematic data-mining angle appears open. One experienced moth-er estimates that "every year, iNaturalist observations generate about 10 species that are new to both Ontario and Canada, provided an expert confirms the ID" ([Feralia](https://feralia.substack.com/p/discovering-rare-moths-with-inaturalist)); 2025–26 firsts include a crayfish "never before reported in Canada" at Ojibway Prairie ([Ontario Parks](https://blog.ontarioparks.ca/inaturalist-at-ontario-parks/); [Canadian Field-Naturalist](https://canadianfieldnaturalist.ca/index.php/cfn/article/view/2223)), a globally rare macrolichen new for Canada ([iNaturalist Canada](https://www.inaturalist.ca/projects/canada-s-groundbreaking-observations)), and a Pacific Golden-Plover at Oshawa that was Ontario's first record, identified from photos only after the bird had left ([ABA](https://www.aba.org/ontario-fall-2025/)) — proof that retrospective re-identification works. For audio, a US user "managed to find first documented state records for three species and three states" in one week of browsing cricket recordings ([iNaturalist post](https://www.inaturalist.org/posts/115259)), and a new cricket species was described after iNaturalist observations ([Zootaxa](https://mapress.com/zt/article/view/47497)); no Ontario audio-mining effort was found. **Data and method.** Pull all Ontario observations (research-grade and needs-ID) through the iNaturalist and GBIF APIs; compute per-taxon novelty against provincial checklists and the GBIF range hull; triage photos with a vision model and audio with Perch 2.0 embeddings, which support nearest-neighbour search from a single example ([CO/AI](https://getcoai.com/news/google-deepmind-expands-perch-ai-to-track-endangered-wildlife-sounds)); shortlist 50–100 candidates and tag taxon experts. A spin-off is a daily monitor for CFIA-regulated pests: the first live spotted-lanternfly detection in Canada was confirmed at Windsor on 15 September 2026, and CFIA counts public uploads as sightings it "has not always been able to verify" ([CFIA](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/plant-health/invasive-species/insects/spotted-lanternfly/observations-canada); [blogTO](https://www.blogto.com/city/2026/09/spotted-lanternfly-ontario-confirmed/)). **Verification.** Expert identification turns an upload into a GBIF-visible first record; the Canadian Field-Naturalist publishes first-record notes; CFIA confirmation is official for pests, and such finds reliably get CBC and blogTO coverage. **Main risk.** Expert bandwidth, model bias on under-represented taxa ([Xeno-canto forum](https://www.xeno-canto.org/forum/topic/44774)), and geoprivacy rules for species at risk.

### 8. Re-scanning 2021–2026 supplementary spreadsheets for gene-name corruption

**Discovery.** Whether Excel's date-conversion of gene names (SEPT2 to "2-Sep") and kindred corruptions persist after HGNC renamed the worst genes and after Microsoft's autocorrect toggle, with a per-journal and per-institution league table including Canadian-affiliated papers. **Already done?** Some competition, fresh by timing. Mark Ziemann's group screened PMC supplements for 2014–2020 and found gene-name errors in **30.9 % (3,436 of 11,117) of articles with supplementary Excel gene lists**, worse than the ~20 % of its 2016 scan ([PLoS Computational Biology via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/8b429b7b5d454a8c9aeef110207e3418)); both scans drew international coverage. No 2024–26 update was found, so "still happening in 2026" is a new result by construction. **Data and method.** Bulk-download PMC open-access supplementary files; parse xlsx and csv; detect date-formatted cells in gene columns; extend to leading-zero loss in identifiers and locale decimal errors; verify a sample by hand with Claude Code doing the parsing. **Verification.** Journals issue corrections; Ziemann at Deakin is the natural external validator and HGNC the authority on names. **Main risk.** Low ethical risk (honest errors, no fraud framing), but newsworthiness depends on the rate being non-trivial; if the toggle fixed it, the story is "the fix worked", which is publishable but not a headline.

### 9. Closing Stephen Leacock's "Lost Leads"

**Discovery.** Newspaper and magazine pieces that Carl Spadoni's 1998 bibliography lists as known-but-unlocated, plus unrecorded first appearances or reprints in Canadian papers to 1930. **Already done?** Some competition: Spadoni's bibliography, with its "Lost Leads" section and a directory of more than 200 newspaper accounts, is the standard ([ECW Press](https://ecwpress.com/products/bibliography-of-stephen-leacock)), and McGill holds a notebook listing ~225 Leacock articles in 67 publications from 1901–1925 ([Fontanus](https://fontanus.library.mcgill.ca/article/view/92/104)), but no post-1998 digital sweep was reported. The method has a strong amateur precedent: Brian Cleary found a Bram Stoker story unknown to biographers for 130 years in an 1890 Dublin newspaper supplement ([National Library of Ireland](https://www.nli.ie/news-stories/news/hidden-bram-stoker-story-unearthed-nlis-archives-amateur-researcher)), and Walt Whitman's lost novel was found by searching character names in a newspaper database ([UNL](https://research.unl.edu/blog/roots-of-whitmans-lost-novel-discovery-traced-to-nebraska)). **Data and method.** Build a target list from "Lost Leads" (title, approximate date, venue); generate OCR-tolerant fuzzy queries (title variants, first lines, signature, known pseudonyms); batch-search Canadiana's serials, OurOntario's Tesseract-OCR'd community papers, Internet Archive and HathiTrust; confirm on page images. **Verification.** Spadoni and McGill Rare Books; the Leacock Museum in Orillia; publication in Papers of the Bibliographical Society of Canada or Canadian Literature; Orillia and Toronto press have an obvious hometown angle. **Main risk.** The Toronto Star and Globe are ProQuest-licensed with text-mining terms unverified, so bulk scraping would breach terms and is the single biggest project-killer; Canadiana currently lacks full-text search and an API for most newspapers, with a rebuild planned ([CRKN](https://www.crkn-rcdr.ca/en/future-canadiana)); "lost" items may sit in titles nobody has digitised.

### 10. Single-transit, long-period planet candidates in TESS full-frame images

**Discovery.** A 50–500-day giant or sub-Neptune candidate that the SPOC and QLP pipelines never flagged because only one transit was seen. **Already done?** Some competition, and the route is demonstrably still producing new planets credited to volunteers: **TOI-6692 b, a ~130-day eccentric Jupiter, was spotted by Visual Survey Group volunteers as a single transit in Sector 39**, went community TOI → TOI → confirmed, with several amateur astronomers and citizen scientists as authors on the paper accepted in January 2026 ([arXiv 2601.16357](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.16357)); TOI-4465 b followed the same path ([Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/worldwide-team-of-citizen-scientists-help-confirm-a-tricky-exoplanet)). The competition is an organised group plus Planet Hunters TESS's new deep-learning model trained on volunteer labels ([AJ](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-3881/add46d)). **Data and method.** TESS-SPOC and QLP FFI light curves from MAST via lightkurve, concentrating on Extended Mission 3 sectors (from September 2025, the least picked-over); detrend; search for isolated box-shaped dips of 4–20 hours in bright dwarf stars; vet with centroid shift, odd–even, momentum-dump timing and Gaia neighbours; estimate period from duration and stellar density. **Verification.** Community TOIs are assigned by independent researchers and promoted to TOIs by the TESS team ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transiting_Exoplanet_Survey_Satellite)); more than 8,000 candidates are on ExoFOP ([NASA Exoplanet Archive](https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/)); co-authorship follows confirmation. **Main risk.** Scattered light, momentum dumps and blended eclipsing binaries mimic single events, and validation is slow; this is the astronomy project with the longest path from candidate to credited result, which is why it ranks below the three above.

### 11. The Ontario emergency-department wait-time series break

**Discovery.** Whether any 2026 report, news article or political statement compared Ontario emergency-department times across a break that the data publisher itself says invalidates comparison, and how Ontario Health, CIHI and think-tank figures reconcile. **Already done?** Open niche by timing. Ontario Health's public "Time Spent in Emergency Departments" page states that "as of the December 2025 data refresh, 43 additional sites were added ... Data exported after this time should not be compared to any data exported earlier" ([Ontario Health](https://ontariohealth.ca/system/reporting/performance/time-spent-in-emergency-departments)); five months later a CCPA study reported that 90 % of Ontario ER patients waited up to 4.5 hours in 2024/25, up from 2.7 hours in 2020/21 ([CP24](https://www.cp24.com/local/toronto/2026/05/11/under-immense-strain-wait-times-at-ontario-emergency-rooms-have-spiked-over-past-5-years-study-says/)), and CIHI has long warned that inter-jurisdictional wait-time differences may reflect definitions rather than real waits ([CMAJ](https://www.cmaj.ca/content/182/8/E340)). No series-break check was found. **Data and method.** Scrape monthly exports before and after the break (the Wayback Machine for pre-refresh tables); compute hospital-level deltas; search the news archive and Hansard for cross-break comparisons; document. **Verification.** Ontario Health and CIHI data teams; health-policy faculty at U of T's IHPME; the outlets that published comparisons. **Main risk.** Aggregate data only (the record-level ERNI dataset is restricted), so the ethical risk is low, but the result is politically sensitive and may be "nobody misused it", which is a methods note rather than a headline. A sibling project with the same timing logic is cross-correlating Health Canada's national wastewater drug dashboard, launched December 2025 with quarterly updates ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/news/2025/12/health-canada-launches-national-wastewater-drug-surveillance-dashboard-to-inform-overdose-crisis-response-and-to-share-data-on-drug-use-in-canada.html)), against Toronto overdose and EMS counts; nobody outside government has had time to do it.

### 12. Handwriting recognition on Ontario's untranscribed rural diaries

**Discovery.** First-hand accounts of datable events — the 1918 influenza, the 1913 Great Lakes storm, eclipses, prices, first cars and telephones — in diaries nobody has read since they were digitised, turned into a queryable dataset. **Already done?** Open niche. The University of Guelph's Rural Diary Archive holds roughly 550 digitised Ontario diaries, each tagged "transcribed" or "untranscribed" with a progress field and an open volunteer-typing pipeline ([Rural Diary Archive](https://ruraldiaries.lib.uoguelph.ca/transcribe/items/browse)); no HTR pass was found. Institutions are doing bulk HTR, but on their own priority fonds: CRKN and Library and Archives Canada transcribed ~600,000 pages of Indian Affairs letterbooks with Transkribus at a 5–7 % character error rate on handwriting ([CRKN](https://www.crkn-rcdr.ca/en/departmental-letterbooks-fully-transcribed-through-intelligent-character-recognition-icr-pilot)), and CRKN's Héritage collection holds 41 million pages, about 60 % handwritten ([CRKN](https://crkn-rcdr.ca/en/crkn-launches-pilot-transcription-project)). **Data and method.** Run Transkribus public models, TrOCR or Claude vision on page images; extract people, places, weather, illness and prices with an LLM; cross-check against OurOntario newspapers and FamilySearch vital records for the same day. **Verification.** Guelph's project staff review transcriptions through the existing volunteer channel; local historical societies and Ontario History. **Main risk.** Image reuse terms were not confirmed; findings may be "charming" rather than newsworthy unless tied to a known event — so pick the diaries that span 1913 and 1918 first.

### 13. Settling the authorship of the "Canadian Boat-Song" by stylometry

**Discovery.** A quantitative ranking of the six to eight claimed authors of the anonymous 1829 Blackwood's poem — Galt, Moir, Lockhart, Wilson, Scott, Dunlop — against register-matched verse corpora, the first computational contribution to a 195-year-old debate. **Already done?** Open (unverified): every published argument (Dowler, Waterston, Needler) is archival and qualitative ([Canadian Poetry](https://canadianpoetry.org/volumes/vol6/dowler.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadian_Boat-Song)), and no computational study surfaced, but the specific "Boat-Song + stylometry" query could not be run. **Data and method.** All candidate corpora are public domain on Project Gutenberg, Internet Archive and HathiTrust; Burrows's Delta and the General Imposters method with bootstrapped short-text controls; publish the code. **Verification.** The editors of Canadian Poetry at Western, Scottish-literature scholars, and the Wikipedia talk page; the Globe and Mail has covered the poem before. **Main risk.** Thirty-two short lines give low statistical power, register variation explains much of what stylometry finds ([Grieve](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/183727630/10.1515_cllt_2022_0040.pdf)), and the honest output is "most consistent with X", not proof. Cheap, quick and niche-outlet-sized; a good second project.

### 14. Toronto's light-pollution trend, ward by ward, from VIIRS

**Discovery.** Which Toronto and GTA wards brightened or dimmed between 2012 and 2026, and whether the city's LED streetlight conversion produced the apparent "dimming" that VIIRS records in Europe. **Already done?** Open niche: no Toronto VIIRS study was found, only global work showing VIIRS radiance rising ~2.2 % a year while Globe at Night star counts imply ~9.6 %, a gap attributed to LED spectral shifts the satellite cannot see ([arXiv 2409.13111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13111)). **Data and method.** Free NASA/NOAA VIIRS Day/Night Band monthly composites, City of Toronto ward boundaries and streetlight data from the open-data portal; aggregate radiance by ward and year; locate the LED-conversion signature. **Verification.** RASC Toronto Centre, DarkSky chapters and U of T astronomy. **Main risk.** VIIRS blindness to blue LED light can understate real sky-glow, so the write-up must carry the Globe at Night caveat; and the result is a map, not an object. Its virtue is speed: two to four weeks to a complete, checkable, locally newsworthy first result, which makes it the best warm-up for learning the gatekeeper loop.

### 15. New cage-graph records

**Discovery.** A smaller k-regular graph of girth g than the current best for an open (k, g) case, lowering the upper bound on n(k, g). **Already done?** Some competition: in November 2025 Exoo, Goedgebeur, Jooken, Stubbe and Van den Eede improved eleven classical cage cases, including n(4,10) ≤ 320 from a 384 that had stood for 22 years ([arXiv 2511.07247](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07247v1); [EJC](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/view/v33i3p48)), and the cubic girth-23 to 32 records were set with Cayley-graph and voltage-lift constructions ([EJC](https://www.combinatorics.org/ojs/index.php/eljc/article/view/v30i1p57)); cases (3,13), (3,14) and (3,15) remain structurally open ([arXiv 2503.07400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.07400)). **Data and method.** The House of Graphs cages directory; nauty, SAT solvers and local search with group-theoretic constructions — exactly MAT301 material. **Verification.** A graph file is checked in seconds for degree, girth and order; upload to House of Graphs; a short arXiv note; EJC publishes such records. **Main risk.** The Ghent–Exoo group has more compute and experience, and the remaining cases may need new constructions rather than search. It ranks last on the shortlist because the odds are modest, but it is the project whose success would be most unambiguously the student's own mathematics.

## Start with the meteor orbits, build the Gaia pipeline in parallel, and save precovery for the big swing

The recommended sequence weighs the hard calendar against learning curves. Spend the first week on a warm-up that teaches the whole loop end to end — the VIIRS ward trend (#14) or a batch of new ZTF variables submitted to VSX, the route an amateur used to get **50 new variable stars accepted in one batch in November 2024** ([Cloudy Nights](https://www.cloudynights.com/forums/topic/943451-my-first-major-discovery-50-new-variable-stars%EF%BC%81/)) — because the hand-off to a moderator is the part no tutorial covers. Then make the Global Meteor Network project (#1) the term's main effort: it is local, the gatekeeper is reachable, the field produces new designations every year, and the mathematics is clustering and orbital dynamics rather than astrophysics the student lacks. In parallel, because the date is fixed, build and test the Gaia DR4 pipeline (#3) on DR3 and the June 2026 pre-release so it runs on 2 December 2026. Clear the Minor Planet Center's archival-submission verification (#2) early even if the first precovery attempt comes later, since a newsworthy target appears on its own schedule and the credit per find is the largest on the list. The Erdős certificate (#6) and cages (#15) are the projects to run when a problem set makes the mathematics fresh; the Ontario quarry-blast catalogue (#4) and the ravine LiDAR survey (#5) are the best pure-local stories and need no astronomy at all, which makes either of them the right choice if the student would rather be in The Varsity and the Toronto Star than in an MPEC.

## Tempting dead ends: where the evidence says not to spend a term

The common thread among the dead ends is that the search space is either already swept by better-resourced actors, physically inaccessible from a laptop, or unverifiable in principle. **Repeating the Rabtsevich recipe** — shallow short-period transits in 2-minute TESS light curves of bright stars — is the most crowded amateur niche in astronomy, with the news story, replication repositories and professional uniform-vetting catalogues all in the same parameter space ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/did-claude-help-an-amateur-astronomer-discover-a-new-planet-2000824393); [arXiv 2606.13789](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.13789)). **Prompting a model to prove an Erdős problem** is where labs and hobbyists are already racing at a 1–2 % hit rate, and the dominant outcome is rediscovering a published solution, which is exactly how OpenAI embarrassed itself in October 2025 ([Gary Marcus](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/erdosgate)). **Famous ciphers** are closed in different ways: the Kryptos K4 plaintext was found in Jim Sanborn's archived papers in September 2025, sold at auction, and now sits behind a buyer-built checker that charges $1 per guess ([Royal Examiner](https://royalexaminer.com/the-cias-35-year-old-puzzle-was-cracked-by-accident/); [Paradigm](https://www.paradigm.xyz/writing/kryptos)); the Zodiac Z13 and Z32 are too short for any solution to be verifiable, in the words of the man who led the Z340 solve ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2021/06/23/world/ive-cracked-zodiac-french-engineer-says-online-sleuths-are-skeptical)); the Beale ciphers are argued to be a hoax ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsolved!)); the Voynich LLM angle was taken by a 2026 preprint ([arXiv 2609.20835](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20835)); and the Indus script carries a US$1 million prize that guarantees funded competition ([Archaeology Magazine](https://archaeologymag.com/2025/01/prize-offered-to-decipher-indus-valley-script/)). Tomokiyo's Cryptiana list was swept in six weeks ([AFP via eNCA](https://www.enca.com/lifestyle/mixed-feelings-ai-cracks-centuries-old-ciphers)).

**Number-theory records** belong to supercomputers: Collatz was verified to 2^71 in January 2025 on "thousands of parallel workers across several European supercomputers" ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11227-025-07337-0)); the October 2024 Mersenne prime "ends the 28-year run of ordinary personal computers finding these huge primes" ([GIMPS](https://www.mersenne.org/primes/press/M136279841.html)); the Wall–Sun–Sun search ran on PrimeGrid for two years without a find ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall%E2%80%93Sun%E2%80%93Sun_prime)). Exact R(5,5), the value of BB(6), and the tables AlphaEvolve and FunSearch already optimised are in the same category ([arXiv 2409.15709](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2409.15709); [BB(6) wiki](https://wiki.bbchallenge.org/wiki/BB(6)); [alphaXiv](https://alphaxiv.org/overview/2511.02864v2)). **AI painting attribution** is a contested commercial field whose own claims — a Caravaggio at "85.7 %", a Rubens disputed by the Centrum Rubenianum — are rejected by connoisseurs, and vision-language models "have limited capabilities to perform canvas attribution" ([Hyperallergic](https://hyperallergic.com/1045583/ai-company-says-it-has-outsmarted-caravaggio-experts/); [Artnet](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/rubens-attribution-ai-authentication-2622500); [arXiv 2508.01408](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2508.01408)). **Image-duplication sleuthing** has the richest press record (Sholto David's post led to seven Dana-Farber retractions and a US$15 million settlement) but it is tool-gated by paid software, legally exposed, and the worst fit for an unsupervised undergraduate; PubPeer's rule that "an image looks rearranged" is acceptable while "authors deliberately pasted in a band" is not shows how narrow the safe path is ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sholto_David); [PubPeer](https://pubpeer.com/static/misconduct); [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/litigation/sarkar-v-does/)). **Unidentified-remains identification** is police-partnered genetic genealogy, not newspaper mining ([DNA Doe Project](https://dnadoeproject.org/case/nation-river-lady-jane-doe-1975)). **Computational drug candidates without a lab partner** are portfolio projects, not discoveries; COVID Moonshot's volunteers were mostly professional chemists backed by a funded synthesis pipeline ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/11/10/1083222/covid-moonshot-drug-discovery-open-source/amp/)). Smaller traps: **pulsar searching** has no laptop-sized public search-mode data and Pulsar Seekers has yet to credit a discovery ([Einstein@Home](https://einsteinathome.org/content/update-%E2%80%9Ceinsteinhome-pulsar-seekers%E2%80%9D)); **Zooniverse asteroid clicking** yields Isolated Tracklet File entries, not personal designations ([Zooniverse](https://www.zooniverse.org/projects/fulsdavid/the-daily-minor-planet/about/results)); **exoplanet transit-timing re-analysis** is professional territory and amateur timings were already judged too noisy in 2015 ([MNRAS](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/450/3/3101/1066663)); Gaia DR3 binaries and Kepler planets are over-mined; Toronto parking tickets and TTC delay logs are thoroughly mined; shipwreck and crater hunting need sonar or drill cores; lost-film discovery needs physical archives; Ontario's Toronto Telegram is simply not digitised ([Genealogy à la carte](https://genealogyalacarte.ca/?p=42536)).

## How to actually run one: the playbook the precedents wrote

Every credited amateur case, and every embarrassing failure, followed the same four moves in order; the successes did all four and the failures skipped one.

**Prove it is not already known before writing a line of search code.** The dominant outcome of AI-assisted Erdős attempts is rediscovery, and Bloom's "open" status "simply meant he was unaware of published solutions" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45633482)). Rabtsevich's defence was a search across 36 catalogues and 340,505 automated alerts before he submitted anything ([GitHub gist](https://gist.github.com/MrBoostie/b7a34b9ec96fbfb37e8fd250ba6debba)). For each project the equivalent is specific: the IAU Meteor Data Center list and last two GMN annual reports; the MPC's object page and all MPECs; VSX, Gaia's variability tables and the ZTF periodic catalogue; the erdosproblems.com forum thread and the problem's references; the iNaturalist project journals and provincial checklists; Spadoni's bibliography; the "needs ID" state of every candidate observation. Post the literature check publicly (on the forum thread, in the preregistration) so it is part of the record, and keep a provenance log of every source consulted, because the Anthropic–Copenhagen enzyme dispute shows that "who knew what, when" becomes the story if priority is contested ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/28/1145230/when-can-we-say-ai-made-a-scientific-discovery/)).

**Run a search-then-falsify loop with adversarial, cross-vendor review.** The distinctive AI feature of the exoplanet case was not that Claude Code found the signal; it was that one agent built while "Codex and separate clean agent sessions" reviewed and the human "decided which tests to run, judged whether a result was strong enough, and chose what to keep" across 74 attempts to disprove the hypothesis ([GitHub gist](https://gist.github.com/MrBoostie/b7a34b9ec96fbfb37e8fd250ba6debba); [Cybernews](https://cybernews.com/tech/claude-code-possible-exoplanet-found-in-nasa-data/)). The practical pattern is leave-one-out prediction ("fit on other data, predict hidden transits, and check only those windows"), standard domain tools rather than home-made statistics (TRICERATOPS, Gaia, pixel localisation; D-criteria for showers; Burrows's Delta with short-text controls; GRIM with hand verification of every flag), and a fresh agent session instructed to find the flaw in the write-up. A solo mathematician who did this credited "an adversarial review by Claude" with catching a wrong inequality and a wrong constant before publication ([arXiv 2504.02795](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.02795)). Expect the audit to remove claims; Rabtsevich's did.

**Preregister, freeze and version.** Before the TESS re-observation, Rabtsevich "published fixed predictions and decision rules" — nine expected transits with depths, outcome categories of "recovered, inconsistent, excluded, or insufficient data", and "frozen code and checksums" on Zenodo ([GitHub gist](https://gist.github.com/MrBoostie/b7a34b9ec96fbfb37e8fd250ba6debba)). The equivalents are a Zenodo record with the frozen pipeline before Gaia DR4 drops; a stated significance threshold and sporadic-background model before clustering meteor orbits; a declared candidate list before emailing taxon experts; a Lean certificate whose statement is fixed before the search runs. The stories that travelled furthest had a scheduled, checkable next event; preregistration is what creates one.

**Hand the result to the domain's gatekeeper, in the gatekeeper's format, and let them say it.** Amateur discovery with AI is "amateur detection, professional confirmation". The formats are concrete: MPC submissions need observer, measurer and observatory-code header lines and multi-position tracklets ([MPC](https://www.minorplanetcenter.net/iau/info/ObsDetails.html)); VSX needs period, epoch, range, type, a phased plot and the catalogue cross-check, with moderators who coach ([AAVSO forum](https://forums.aavso.org/t/how-to-vsx-and-variable-star-discovery/1490)); TNS registration is open to amateurs and accepts archival non-detections ([TNS](https://www.wis-tns.org/content/tns-getting-started)); OEIS wants a program, a b-file and a reference ([OEIS](https://oeis.org/Submit.html)); cipher claims go to Cryptologia or Schmeh's blog with key, method and code, not to the press; Ontario archaeological candidates go privately to archaeology@ontario.ca ([ontario.ca](https://www.ontario.ca/page/archaeology)); research-integrity flags go to authors first, then editors, then PubPeer as checkable facts without intent claims ([PubPeer](https://pubpeer.com/static/misconduct)). Write the public post only after the gatekeeper has acted, lead with the caveats ("not statistically validated"), and link the preregistration. Local coverage then follows the institutional hook — telescope time, a designation, a journal note, a museum's acknowledgement — not the AI.

Two constraints sit outside the four moves. Legal: Ontario site locations are confidential, fieldwork needs a licence, and the Ontario Archaeological Society cites fines up to $50,000 for individuals ([OAS](https://ottawa.ontarioarchaeology.on.ca/Don%27tLoot.pdf)); ProQuest-licensed newspapers cannot be bulk-scraped; iNaturalist geoprivacy obscures species at risk. Ethical: aggregate rather than individual findings in health and integrity work, and no publication of suspected burial locations. Both are cheaper to check in the first week than to discover in the last.

## What the researchers could not verify, and which ratings rest on absence of evidence

The six research notes behind this report were compiled under severe tooling limits, and the reader should know exactly where that bites. The Firecrawl scraping connector was out of credits throughout; the shared web-search budget ran out mid-task in every note (roughly 35–45 queries each), leaving five to ten planned confirmatory searches per domain unrun; and the sandbox's outbound proxy refused direct fetches of nearly every non-search host, including Scientific American, Quanta, Euronews, Zenodo, Reddit, erdosproblems.com, the Hacker News API, GitHub's API, MIT Technology Review, the Ontario Data Catalogue, Guelph's Rural Diary Archive, CRKN and Canadiana. The single primary document read in full was the Rabtsevich GitHub gist. Every other fact in this report comes from search-engine extracts of the cited pages, which is why the citations should be opened before any figure is repeated.

Several specific details are therefore unconfirmed: the third agent's name ("Jev"), the Hacker News thread's 49 points, the "ten more planets with Opus 5.5" post, the "80 minutes" for Erdős #1196, the "GPT-6 Astra" designation for the cipher model, and the "700 relations found with Claude" OEIS claim all rest on single secondary sources. Data-access facts marked as prior knowledge in the notes — the GMN daily orbit download and its CC-BY licence, IRSA and NOIRLab archive endpoints, the Canadian Astronomy Data Centre's solar-system image search, Pan-STARRS cutouts, VSX's formal acceptance criteria, Sungrazer's reporting rules — were not verified and must be checked first.

More important for anyone about to invest months: the following "open niche" or "open (unverified)" ratings rest on absence of results in the queries that could be run, not on a confirmed absence of prior work. In Toronto and Ontario: the ravine LiDAR survey, the quarry-blast catalogue, the VIIRS ward trend, the iNaturalist phenology study, the abandoned-mine cross-check, the lost-burial-grounds register, the Scarborough Bluffs update, and the ED series-break and wastewater-concordance audits. In history: Boat-Song stylometry, Rural Diary Archive HTR, the codebook hunt, the Shing Wah index and the newspaper-fiction database. In life science: the Canadian statcheck sweep, the post-2020 Excel re-scan, the Ontario audio first-records hunt and the Perch label audit. In astronomy: DASCH light curves of recent transients, K2 period recovery, and whether the 2025 meteor showers already have parent bodies. In mathematics: Kaplan's Heesch-number tables by polyform size, and the exact open status of any chosen Erdős problem. The one-day re-check before each is a Google Scholar and OpenAlex search on the exact topic, a read of the domain registry's change log (IAU MDC, MPECs, VSX, erdosproblems.com, OEIS recent additions, the Ontario Archaeological Society's newsletters), and an email to the named gatekeeper asking whether anyone is already on it — which, in every precedent, is also the first step of the hand-off.

## Conclusion

The amateur-plus-AI wave has not made discovery easier so much as it has relocated it: the scarce resource is no longer the ability to write the pipeline, which Claude Code supplies, but the judgement to pick an archive whose automated detection threshold leaves residuals, the discipline to prove nobody has looked there, and a relationship with the person whose signature turns a candidate into a record. That is why the strongest ideas here are unglamorous registries — meteor-shower designations, MPC measurer credits, VSX entries, Lean certificates, a provincial first record on GBIF — rather than planets, and why the Toronto-specific projects that look least like "science" (quarry blasts on a seismometer, a 19th-century mill race under a ravine, a wait-time series break) are the ones with the clearest path to a local byline. The TESS Sector 110 result due after 26 November 2026 will decide whether the flagship case becomes a validated precedent or a cautionary tale, and the Gaia DR4 release six days later opens the one window on this list that closes on its own; a student who has cleared the MPC's verification, built a Gaia pipeline on the pre-release data, and emailed Western's meteor group before December will be positioned regardless of which way the flagship falls.
